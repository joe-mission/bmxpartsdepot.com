Buying Guide

BMX Tech & Sizing FAQ

These are the ten questions people actually ask before they buy. Where there is a sourced answer it is here with its sourcing. Where the honest answer is that it depends on the rider, or that the sources disagree, this page says so rather than inventing a number to fill the gap.

All Eras

Quick Answer

The three questions worth answering first: a 20 inch wheel is near enough universal on freestyle BMX, frame size is set by top tube length rather than by wheel size, and the spec sheet tells you more about a complete bike than the price does. Everything below expands on one of those.

Sizing and fit

Q01What size BMX bike do I need for my height?

On a modern freestyle BMX the wheel is 20 inch whatever your height, and the size you actually choose is the top tube length. Adult bikes run roughly 20 to 21.25 inches of top tube. Race BMX is the opposite: it runs a named size class, and the classes really do map to rider height.

That difference catches people out, so it is worth being clear about which question you are asking.

Freestyle. The one figure the sources agree on is that a 20.5 inch top tube suits a rider around 5 feet 4 inches to 5 feet 8 inches. Dan's Comp and Supercross both publish that range and both give the same numbers. Confirmed Below and above that the published bands widen and start disagreeing with each other, so treat them as overlapping suggestions rather than a lookup.

Race. Race brands and retailers publish size charts by height, and this is where it gets awkward. The two ends of the range are solid: Pro tops out at 5 feet 8 inches and Pro XL at 6 feet, and every source checked agrees on both. Confirmed The middle does not agree at all.

Read that sourcing column, because it is the useful part. On Expert, the most common youth size, Dan's Comp and Source BMX say 5 feet 0 inches to 5 feet 4 inches while J and R and Supercross say 4 feet 6 inches to 5 feet 2 inches. That is a six inch offset on the same size name. There is no version of this where one clean number is honest.

Two more things worth knowing before you use anybody's chart. Dan's Comp and Source BMX publish character for character identical height ranges across six sizes, so they are one source wearing two hats rather than two independent confirmations. And the size names above Pro XL are not standardised between brands: one maker's Pro XXL is another's Pro L, and Chase runs the naming out to Pro XXXXXL.

So: measure the top tube, not the label. The named sizes are in the glossary under Junior and Pro XL, and the geometry that goes with them is on the frame guide.

Q02What size wheel does a BMX bike use?

Twenty inch, and that number means a bead seat diameter of 406mm rather than anything you can measure across the tyre. The trap is that a second 20 inch standard exists at 451mm, mostly on race bikes, and the two do not interchange in either direction.

This one is already answered properly in the glossary rather than here, because it is a fitment standard rather than a buying question. Start with wheel size, then read ISO 406 and ISO 451 for why a tyre that says 20 inch on the sidewall can still be the wrong tyre.

Smaller wheels exist for smaller riders, at 12, 14, 16 and 18 inch. Be careful with the height charts for those: the brands publishing them disagree badly. On a 16 inch wheel one maker says 3 feet 0 inches to 3 feet 8 inches and another says 3 feet 11 inches to 4 feet 6 inches, and those bands barely overlap at all. We are not publishing a kids' wheel size by height figure, because there is not a defensible one to publish.

Q03What crank arm length should I run?

Almost every BMX crank sold is 160, 165, 170 or 175mm, and 170mm is what most complete bikes ship with. Beyond that this is rider preference rather than a specification, and the one height chart that exists is contradicted by the manufacturer's own team riders.

The lengths are easy to state. Odyssey, Eclat, Cult, Kink and Sunday all sell the same four sizes, and none of their product pages carries any guidance on choosing between them. Confirmed Profile Racing is the exception, offering 125mm through 190mm, which reflects its race roots.

Where it falls apart is the recommendation. Dan's Comp publishes a crank length chart by rider height, under a heading that says race. Single source Profile Racing publishes an article naming three of its own team riders with their heights and their chosen lengths: a rider at 6 feet 3 inches on 170mm, one at 6 feet 0 inches on 170mm, and one at 6 feet 4 inches on 165mm. Dan's chart says all three of those riders should be on 180 to 190mm.

So the chart and the manufacturer disagree by 15 to 25mm on riders of a known height. No freestyle brand publishes a crank length recommendation at all, and Dan's Comp's own freestyle sizing guide covers wheel size and top tube while leaving crank length out. That omission is the answer.

What is documented is the trade, and it is simple arithmetic rather than opinion. Supercross puts it as leverage against cadence: a 180mm crank gives about nine percent more torque at the pedal than a 165mm, and the pedal circle is about nine percent longer to travel, so peak spin speed suffers. Single source Profile argues the other way for street riding, that shorter cranks put your feet closer together and let you hop higher and spin more easily both ways. Single source

Both are describing the same trade from opposite ends. Ride what the bike came with for a season before changing it, and if you do change, change one thing.

One hard constraint that is not preference: crank length is tied to your bottom bracket standard through spindle length, so check what your shell takes before ordering. That is on the bottom bracket guide.

What kind of bike

Q04Street, park, dirt or race, what is actually different about the bike?

Race bikes are a different machine. Street, park and dirt bikes are the same machine set up differently. That is the honest split, and most buying guides blur it.

A race bike is built to accelerate out of a gate and corner on a groomed track. It runs a longer top tube for stability at speed, sizes by rider height, usually has a rear brake, and race adds spindle sizes that freestyle never uses. A street or park bike is built to be jumped on, ground along things and landed on badly.

Between street, park and dirt the differences are real but small, and they are setup rather than construction:

  • Street tends toward shorter gearing, pegs, and often no brakes at all. Tyres run harder.
  • Park is close to street, with slightly softer tyres for transition grip and more riders keeping a brake.
  • Dirt runs a slightly longer back end for stability in the air and softer tyres for loose ground, and dirt riders keep brakes more often than street riders do.

A used bike sold as one of these is usually just a bike with somebody's preferences on it. The frame underneath is the thing to judge, and the frame guide covers what actually differs in the geometry.

Q05Freestyle and race BMX, are they the same bike?

No, and the clearest tell is the size chart. Freestyle is near enough one size with a 20 inch wheel and roughly 20 to 21.25 inches of top tube. Race is a graded set of named sizes fitted to rider height, from Micro up through Pro XXL.

Underneath that there are real mechanical differences. Race runs ISO 451 wheels in places freestyle never does, adds 30mm and 35mm crank spindles that are not part of the freestyle set at all, and builds frames around gate starts rather than around landing on the frame. Freestyle adds pegs, gyros, and a whole set of parts race has no use for.

They share a wheel diameter and a look, which is why they get talked about as one thing. A race frame will not enjoy being used for street, and a street bike will lose a race.

Materials, gearing and brakes

Q06Chromoly or hi-ten steel, is the price difference worth it?

Yes for anything that gets landed on, and the difference is a real material property rather than marketing. Chromoly is roughly twice as strong as hi-ten for the same weight of tube, which is why a chromoly frame can use thinner walls and come out lighter and stronger at once.

Both terms are already defined properly in the glossary, with the strength figures and their sourcing, so this answer points there rather than repeating them: chromoly and hi-ten steel.

The practical part for a buyer is that it is rarely all or nothing. Bikes step up in stages, and the order is consistent across brands: hi-ten everything, then a chromoly front triangle with hi-ten stays, then a full chromoly frame, then a full chromoly fork, then chromoly bars. A listing that says "chromoly" without saying which parts usually means the front triangle only.

If you are buying used and the decals are gone, the frame guide covers how to tell them apart by weight, by the ring when you tap the tube, and by what the welds look like.

Q07What gear ratio should a beginner run, and what does the ratio number mean?

Ride what the bike came with. The ratio is the front sprocket teeth divided by the rear cog teeth, and a modern complete almost always ships at 25/9, which is 2.78 to 1. There is no sourceable "beginner ratio" beyond that, because it is not a specification, it is what suits a rider.

That is worth saying plainly rather than dressing up. The ratios in current use by discipline are published on the drivetrain guide with their sourcing, and 25/9 is the default across modern freestyle completes. What nobody publishes, because it does not exist as a fact, is a recommendation for a beginner specifically.

What the number means is straightforward. A 25 tooth sprocket driving a 9 tooth cog turns the wheel 2.78 times per pedal revolution. A bigger number is a harder gear that goes faster once you are moving and is slower to get going. The reason BMX quotes 25/9 rather than the 44/16 of twenty years ago is ground clearance: the same ratio with fewer teeth means a much smaller sprocket that does not hit things.

Full gearing explanation, including gear inches and why two charts give different numbers for the same teeth, is on the drivetrain guide.

Q08U-brake, caliper, or no brakes at all?

A modern BMX runs a U-brake on the back or nothing. Calipers are not a current BMX part. The real decision is brake or brakeless, and it is not only a style question, because a frame that never had mounts cannot be given them.

The U-brake is the standard, mounting on two posts either side of the rim. Caliper brakes, the kind that reach down around the wheel from a single bolt, belong to road bikes and to very old BMX. You will not find one on a current complete.

Brakeless is genuinely common in street riding, and plenty of frames now ship with no mounts at all. Before buying a frame, look at the seat stays and chain stays: posts mean a brake fits, threaded bosses with bolts in them mean it takes removable mounts and you need to find the mounts, and smooth stays mean brakeless permanently. The brakes guide has the mount dimensions and what goes missing off a used brake set.

One thing worth knowing if you are mixing parts: a lever and a brake have to be built for the same cable pull. A short pull lever on a linear pull brake never gets the pads to the rim, and everything bolts together and looks correct while it does not work. That is covered under cable pull ratio.

Buying

Q09How much should a first real BMX bike cost, and what do you get at each price point?

We are not publishing a price tier table, because the spec is what you are buying and the price does not track it. At around the same money you can buy an all hi-ten bike from one brand and a chromoly framed one from another. Check the spec sheet instead, and the ladder below is the thing to check it against.

That is a deliberate choice rather than a gap. Dan's Comp, the largest BMX retailer with live pricing in front of them, publishes a bike buyers guide that discusses hi-ten against chromoly, sealed bearings and freecoasters and attaches no prices at all. When the people with the data decline to publish tiers, a reference site repeating tiers from memory is inventing precision.

What is durable is the order things improve in. Every brand checked steps up in the same sequence, and every step is printed on the spec sheet:

  • Frame material, in stages: hi-ten frame, fork and bars, then a chromoly front triangle, then a full chromoly frame, then a chromoly fork, then chromoly bars.
  • Bottom bracket: unsealed American loose ball first, then a sealed Mid.
  • Rear hub: unsealed, then a sealed cassette, then a freecoaster near the top.

One piece of standard advice is now out of date and worth correcting. "Make sure it has three piece cranks" no longer filters anything: no BMX specialist brand checked sells a one piece crank at any price, and the cheapest 20 inch complete from a BMX brand already has three piece chromoly cranks. The boundary only still exists inside general sporting goods brands, at the very bottom of the range. Check the bottom bracket and the rear hub instead, which still discriminate.

As a single dated anchor rather than a table: as of August 2026, complete 20 inch bikes from BMX specialist brands started at around 400 US dollars, and brand price floors varied by about 50 percent between makers. Single source That figure will go stale, the spec ladder above will not.

Q10Can I run tubeless on a BMX?

Usually not, and it needs both the rim and the tyre to be built for it rather than just sealant in a normal setup. The requirement is a rim bed that seals and a bead that locks, and most BMX rims and tyres are neither.

This is a glossary term rather than a buying question, so the detail lives there: tubeless BMX.

The short buying answer is that on a used wheel you should assume tubed unless the rim is explicitly sold as tubeless ready, and that BMX has far less tubeless adoption than mountain biking for a reason: the impacts are harder, the pressures are higher, and burping a bead on a landing is worse than a pinch flat.
