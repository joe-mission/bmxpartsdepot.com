Frame Identification, Materials and Geometry

A used BMX frame usually turns up with no spec sheet, no box and half its stickers gone. Everything you need is on the frame itself. This guide covers what to look at, what each answer means, and where a figure has not been checked it says so instead of filling the gap.

Old SchoolMid SchoolModern

Quick Answer

Start with the sticker or the decal on the seat tube. A frame that says 4130, chromoly or cro-mo is chromoly steel. A frame with no material claim anywhere on it is usually hi-ten, because hi-ten is not a thing brands advertise.

After that, the frame tells you the rest by hand: pick it up, look at the dropouts, look for brake mounts, and put a tape on the top tube. Those four checks separate almost every frame you will meet.

How to identify a used BMX frame

Work in order, cheapest check first, and stop as soon as you have what you need.

Read what is written on it. Seat tube decals, head tube badges, dropout stampings and the underside of the bottom bracket shell are the four places brands put information. A frame can be stripped of every sticker and still carry a stamp on a dropout or a serial under the shell.

Pick it up. Weight is the single most useful thing your hands tell you about a bare frame. A chromoly frame and a hi-ten frame of the same size and shape are not close, and once you have handled a few of each you will call it correctly nearly every time. This is a comparison, not a measurement. It works when you have both in the shop and it fails on the phone.

Look at the bottom bracket shell. The shell diameter puts the frame in a rough era and tells you which cranks it takes. That whole subject has its own guide.

Look at the dropouts. Slot width, whether the slot is horizontal or vertical, whether the dropouts are plate or investment cast, and whether there are chain tensioner provisions. These are era and quality markers as much as fitment ones.

Look for brake mounts. Present, absent, or bolt-on. This is a yes or no answer that changes what the frame is worth to a given buyer more than most geometry numbers do.

Then measure. Top tube, chainstay, standover, wheelbase. Nobody needs all four, but the top tube is non-negotiable because it is how every frame is listed.

Chromoly, hi-ten and what the sticker means

Quick Answer

4130 chromoly is a chrome-molybdenum steel alloy. Hi-ten is plain high tensile steel. For the same shape and the same strength, chromoly can be made with thinner walls, so a chromoly frame is lighter than the hi-ten equivalent.

If a frame is chromoly, it says so. Brands print 4130, cro-mo, chromoly or the tubing supplier's name on the frame, because it is the thing they are selling.

Chromoly is not automatically better in every way. It is stronger for its weight, which is a different claim. A thick-walled hi-ten frame from a solid brand will outlive a badly repaired chromoly one, and hi-ten in a low-stress part is a perfectly reasonable material. What hi-ten cannot do is be light.

The term you want underneath all this is yield strength, the stress at which steel stops springing back and stays bent. Chromoly's higher yield strength is why a tube with thinner walls can survive the same hit, and why a dented chromoly frame is more likely to have been hit hard rather than leaned on. Published alloy figures exist but they describe raw tube, not a finished welded frame, so this guide does not quote them as though they told you what a frame will take.

Reading the sticker honestly

A frame can be chromoly in the main triangle and hi-ten everywhere else. "Full chromoly" or "100% chromoly" claims the whole frame including stays and dropouts. A frame that says "chromoly main tubes" is telling you the stays are something cheaper, and that is a real and normal spec, not a con. When you cannot see a claim at all, list it as unmarked rather than guessing.

The magnet test does not do what people think

Both chromoly and hi-ten are steel and both are magnetic. A magnet tells you steel from aluminium, and that is all. It will not separate two steels. On BMX frames this matters less than it sounds, because BMX frames are overwhelmingly steel, but it comes up constantly with cranks, stems, bars and sprockets where aluminium is common.

Butted tubing

Quick Answer

Butted tubing is thicker at the ends and thinner in the middle. The ends are where the welds and the stress concentrations are, so that is where the metal is left; the middle of a tube does comparatively little work and gets thinned to save weight.

A straight-gauge tube has the same wall thickness end to end. To be strong enough at the joints it has to be that thick everywhere, and you carry the extra metal down the whole length for nothing.

The naming counts transitions, not tubes. Single butted has one thickness change. Double butted is thick, thin, thick, which is the common frame tube. Triple butted has three different wall thicknesses. Quad butted claims four. Past double butted the marketing outruns the engineering fairly quickly, and quad butted in particular is used loosely enough by brands that it is worth treating as a description of intent rather than a spec.

You cannot see butting from outside. The outer diameter is constant, which is the point. What you can do is feel it: a butted frame is noticeably lighter than a straight-gauge frame that looks identical, and that weight gap is the honest reason butting exists.

For a used buyer, butting matters in one specific way. A thin-walled middle section dents more easily than a thick-walled one. A dent in the middle of a butted top tube is in the thinnest part of the tube. That is worth knowing before you decide a dent is cosmetic.

Gussets and where frames get reinforced

Quick Answer

A gusset is a plate or tube welded into a joint to spread load away from the weld. The two places they appear on BMX frames are the head tube junction and the bottom bracket area, because those are where the loads arrive.

A weld is a hard line. Load arriving at that line concentrates there, and cracks on steel frames start at welds far more often than in the middle of tubes. A gusset moves the edge of the reinforcement away from the weld and gives the load somewhere gentler to go.

Common forms: a flat plate under the down tube where it meets the head tube, a wraparound plate over the top of the head tube junction, a triangular plate between the down tube and the bottom bracket shell, and a tube gusset that runs from the head tube back to the down tube. Some frames use none, some use several. More gussets does not mean a better frame; a heavier-walled tube in the right place does the same work without them.

Gussets are also a dating cue. Big obvious external plate gussets read older, and smoothly integrated or tube gussets read newer, but the overlap is wide enough that this is a hint rather than a method. Do not date a frame on gussets alone.

The chainstay yoke

Down at the other end, the chainstay yoke is the shaped bridge or plate that joins the two chainstays near the bottom bracket. It is doing structural work, but the thing it decides for a buyer is clearance: how fat a tyre will fit, and how the sprocket sits. A frame with a tight yoke and a frame with a deeply recessed one are not going to take the same tyre, whatever the rest of the geometry says.

Dropouts, slot width and brake mounts

Quick Answer

Dropout slot width decides which axle drops in. BMX rear axles are not all the same diameter, and a frame cut for a narrower axle will not accept a fatter one without filing, which is not a repair we would recommend or sell.

The rear dropout is where a lot of a frame's character sits. Things to look at:

Slot width. A 14mm axle needs a slot at least 14mm wide. A frame built around a smaller axle has a narrower slot. People do file slots open. A filed slot has bright metal, uneven edges and often a bellmouth at the opening, and it has removed material from the most highly loaded part of the dropout.

Slot direction. Standard BMX dropouts have a horizontal, rearward-facing slot so the axle can slide back and forth to tension the chain. A vertical dropout fixes the axle position and needs either an exact chain length, a half link, or a tensioner elsewhere. Vertical dropouts are common on geared bikes and rare on BMX, and if you meet one on a BMX frame it is worth flagging in a listing because it changes the build.

Dropout thickness and construction. Thin plate dropouts flex and eventually crack around the slot. Thick plate or investment cast dropouts do not. Look at the mouth of the slot for spreading or ovalising, which is what happens when an axle has been run loose.

Tensioner provision. Threaded holes behind the dropout for a chain tensioner bolt, or a machined pocket for an internal tensioner, are useful and worth mentioning.

Brake mounts

Whether a frame has brake mounts is a yes or no question with three answers. Welded posts, no posts, or bolt-on removable brake mounts.

Welded U-brake posts on the seat stays are the normal arrangement on frames that expect a rear brake. A frame with no posts anywhere is a brakeless frame, and no amount of wanting will put a rear brake on it cleanly. Removable mounts sit in between: the frame has threaded bosses and the posts bolt on, so it can go either way. If you are selling a frame with removable mounts, the mounts themselves are a small part that is easy to lose and worth listing as present or absent, because a frame without them is functionally brakeless to the buyer.

Cable routing is the other half of this. Look for cable guides on the top tube, a seat stay bridge with a hole for the straddle cable carrier, and gyro tabs on the head tube. A frame with gyro tabs was built expecting a detangler.

Top tube length and how a frame is sized

Quick Answer

Top tube length is the headline size number on every BMX frame, and it is measured horizontally, not along the tube. You measure from the centre of the head tube to the centre of the seat tube along a level line, which is why it is also called effective top tube.

BMX frames are sized by top tube because seat tube length is meaningless on a bike ridden with the seat slammed. A BMX seat tube is short by design and tells you nothing about how the bike fits.

The horizontal part matters. Many BMX frames have a sloping top tube, and if you measure along the tube you get a longer number than the frame is actually sold as. Two frames with the same real length can differ by a useful amount if one is measured along a slope. When you list a frame, say that the measurement is horizontal, centre to centre.

How to take the measurement

Stand the frame up so the bottom bracket and the dropouts are level, or better, put the wheels back in and stand the bike on flat ground. Put a level or a straight edge across from the centre of the head tube to the seat tube, and measure that horizontal line centre to centre. A tape and a level does the job. Precision to the nearest sixteenth of an inch is more than anyone needs.

Choosing a size

Top tube is chosen by rider height and by what the bike is for, and the honest answer is that the ranges overlap heavily. Riders of the same height run different lengths depending on whether they ride street, park or trails, and personal preference moves it further. Anyone publishing a single top tube length for a given height is oversimplifying. This guide is not publishing a height chart until the ranges have been checked against what manufacturers actually recommend rather than against each other.

Frame reach is the more precise version of the same idea: the horizontal distance from the bottom bracket to the top of the head tube. It separates two frames that share a top tube length but put the bottom bracket in different places. It is not printed on many BMX geometry charts, which limits how useful it currently is when comparing used frames.

Chainstay length and head tube angle

Quick Answer

Chainstay length is the distance from the bottom bracket centre to the rear axle centre, and it is the number that decides how easily the front end comes up. Head tube angle is how far the head tube leans back from vertical, and it decides how quickly the bike steers.

Chainstay length

Short chainstays pull the rear wheel in under the rider. Weight shifts back, the front comes up more easily, and the bike turns more sharply. That is why street and park frames trend short. Long chainstays push the wheel back, which makes the bike more stable at speed, easier to land on and better behaved through a corner under power. That is why race frames trend long.

The tradeoffs are real in both directions. Very short stays cost you tyre clearance and can make the bike twitchy under a hard pedal. Long stays make manuals and pulling up hard work. There is no correct number, only a number that suits how the bike is ridden.

Chainstay length also drives chain length, which is why it belongs next to the drivetrain guide rather than sitting alone in a geometry chart.

Head tube angle

A steeper head angle, meaning closer to vertical, gives a shorter trail and a quicker, more nervous steering feel. A slacker angle lays the fork out in front, lengthens trail, and gives a more stable and more self-centring one. Fork offset works with the head angle to set the final result, so the two numbers cannot be read independently.

In practice head angle is one of the less useful numbers when comparing two used BMX frames, because the range across the whole category is narrow and the differences between frames are small. Top tube and chainstay length will tell you far more about how a frame rides.

Standover and wheelbase

Quick Answer

Standover height is how high the top tube sits above the ground at its lowest useful point. Wheelbase is the distance between the front and rear axle centres. Standover is a practical fit check. Wheelbase is a quick way to compare how long two frames actually are once the fork is in.

Standover is straightforward: stand over the bike, see whether the top tube is in the way. On a BMX bike, where the whole point is being able to move around over the frame, it is a check worth doing in person and worth reporting honestly in a listing. Measure it with the wheels and tyres on, because tyre size changes it.

Wheelbase is the more useful comparison number of the two, and it is the one people forget exists. Two frames with the same top tube length can have quite different wheelbases if their chainstays or head angles differ, and wheelbase is what you actually feel. It is measured axle centre to axle centre with the bike built, which is its practical drawback: you cannot take it on a bare frame.

For a bare frame, top tube and chainstay length together get you close enough. Wheelbase is worth measuring when a complete bike comes through and worth putting in the listing, since almost nobody else does.

Serial numbers and dating a frame

Quick Answer

Almost every BMX frame has a serial number stamped on the underside of the bottom bracket shell. Turn the bike upside down and look there first. A BMX serial number will not reliably give you a build year, because there is no shared registry and most brands never published their format.

Other places to look if the shell is blank or the stamping is unreadable: the rear dropouts, the head tube, and occasionally the underside of a chainstay. Old paint and rust hide stampings well, and a wire brush and a torch held at a low angle will bring up a stamping that looks absent head on.

What a serial is genuinely for is proving identity. Record it before you sell a bike, and check it against a stolen bike register before you buy one. That is a real use and worth doing every time.

What a serial is not for is dating. Some brands encoded a date. Many did not. There is no central lookup that covers BMX the way there is for some car and motorcycle marques, and the tables that circulate online for particular brands are usually community-assembled from observed examples rather than published by anyone. They can be useful, and they are not authoritative, and this guide is not going to reprint one as if it were.

Dating a frame is better done by the parts standards it was built around. Bottom bracket shell, head tube and headset type, dropout style, brake mounts and cable routing all moved over time, and a frame that combines a particular set of them sits in a particular window. That is a whole topic and it belongs in the vintage identification guide rather than here.

Checking a used frame for damage

Quick Answer

Look at every weld, look at both dropouts, and sight down the frame from front and back. If something is cracked, bent, or sketchy, it does not get sold. A frame is the one part where a failure puts you on the ground.

Do this in good light with the frame stripped if possible, and take your time.

Welds. Every weld, all the way around. You are looking for a hairline that follows the edge of the weld bead, and for paint that has lifted in a line rather than chipped in a patch. Cracks on steel frames start at welds. The high-load ones are the head tube junctions, the bottom bracket shell where the down tube and seat tube arrive, the chainstay to shell joint, and the dropout to stay joints. Rust that runs in a line rather than sitting in a patch is a crack until proven otherwise.

Dropouts. Both of them, both sides. Look at the mouth of the slot for spreading, at the face for a filed or ground surface, and at the plate for bending. Bent dropouts mean the wheel will never sit straight.

Alignment. Sight down the frame from behind with the seat tube lined up with the head tube. The rear triangle should be square. Put a wheel in and see whether it sits centred between the stays with equal clearance either side. A wheel that sits off-centre is either dished wrong or the frame is bent, and swapping in another wheel tells you which.

Head tube. Look for ovalising, ripples in the down tube just behind the head tube, and paint cracked in a ring around the joint. This is what a hard front impact leaves behind, and it is a frame-ending fault.

Dents. A dent in the middle of a butted tube is in its thinnest section. Small and smooth in a down tube is common and usually cosmetic. Sharp, creased, or accompanied by a ripple in the tube behind it is not.

Repairs. Re-welding, brazing, added plates and filler are all things that turn up on old frames. A repair is not automatically disqualifying, but it is always disclosable, and a repair somebody tried to hide under paint tells you something about the rest of the bike.

Threads and shells. Chase the seat clamp bolt, look inside the bottom bracket shell for a bore that has been reamed or hammered, and check brake mount threads if they are there. Stripped threads on a frame are expensive to fix properly.

Rust. Surface rust on the outside of a steel frame is normal on anything old and is not by itself a reason to pass. Rust from the inside out, showing as bubbling paint or a soft spot, is the end of that tube. Look inside the seat tube and inside the head tube with a torch, because that is where water sits.

Questions

Q081How do I tell if a BMX frame is chromoly or hi-ten?

Look for a claim on the frame. Chromoly is a selling point, so brands print 4130, cro-mo, chromoly or a tubing supplier's name on a seat tube decal or a head tube badge. A frame with no material claim anywhere on it is usually hi-ten, because nobody advertises hi-ten.

If the stickers are gone, weight in hand is the practical test. A chromoly frame is noticeably lighter than a hi-ten frame of the same size and shape. That is a comparison rather than a measurement, so it works when you can handle both and it does not work from a photograph.

Be aware a frame can be chromoly in the main triangle and hi-ten in the stays. "Full chromoly" claims the whole thing. "Chromoly main tubes" does not, and that is a normal spec rather than a trick.

Q083What does 4130 mean on a BMX frame sticker?

4130 is the alloy designation for chrome-molybdenum steel. It is a standard steel grade, not a brand, so any manufacturer can use it and the number on your frame does not tell you who made the tubing.

What it tells you is that the frame is built from an alloy steel with a higher yield strength than plain high tensile steel, which means the same strength can be achieved with thinner tube walls and less weight. It does not tell you the wall thickness, whether the tubes are butted, whether the whole frame is 4130 or only the main triangle, or how well it was welded. All of those matter as much as the alloy.

Q082Does a magnet test tell me anything about a BMX frame?

It tells you steel from aluminium and nothing else. Chromoly and hi-ten are both steel and both magnetic, so a magnet will not separate them.

Where the magnet earns its keep is on components rather than frames. Cranks, stems, bars, sprockets, pegs and rims all come in both steel and aluminium, and a magnet answers that question in a second. On a frame, which is almost always steel on a BMX bike, it confirms what you already assumed.

Q051How do I measure BMX top tube length?

Horizontally, from the centre of the head tube to the centre of the seat tube. Not along the tube.

Stand the bike on level ground, or stand a bare frame so the bottom bracket and dropouts sit level. Lay a level or a straight edge from the head tube centre back to the seat tube and measure along that horizontal line. Many BMX frames have a sloping top tube, so measuring along the tube gives a longer number than the size the frame is sold as.

When you list a frame, say the measurement is horizontal and centre to centre. It removes the most common argument in used frame sales.

Q050What BMX top tube length do I need for my height?

There is no single right answer, and the ranges for any given height overlap heavily. Riders of the same height run different lengths depending on whether they ride street, park, trails or race, and preference moves it further than you would expect.

The useful approach is to measure the top tube on a bike you already get on with and use that as your reference, then move in small steps from there. A shorter top tube feels quicker and easier to throw around. A longer one feels more stable and gives you more room to move.

This guide is not publishing a height chart. The ones online mostly copy each other, and we have not checked the ranges against what manufacturers actually recommend.

Q052What does chainstay length change on a BMX bike?

How easily the front wheel comes up, and how stable the bike is. Short chainstays put the rear wheel closer to the bottom bracket, shift weight rearward, make the front end lift more easily and make the bike turn more sharply. That suits street and park riding.

Longer chainstays push the rear wheel back, which makes the bike more stable at speed, calmer under hard pedalling and easier to land on. That suits racing and trails.

Chainstay length also sets how much chain you need and how much tyre clearance you have, so it is not purely a handling number.

Q054Where is the serial number on a BMX frame?

Underside of the bottom bracket shell, on most frames. Turn the bike over and look there first.

If nothing is there, or the stamping is filled with paint and rust, check the rear dropouts, the head tube and the underside of a chainstay. A wire brush and a torch held at a low angle will bring up a stamping that looks absent when you view it straight on.

Record it before you sell a bike, and check it against a stolen bike register before you buy one. Do not expect it to give you a build year.

Q055How do I tell if a used BMX frame is cracked or has been repaired?

Strip it if you can, get it into good light, and go around every weld. Cracks on steel frames start at welds, so you are looking for a hairline following the edge of a weld bead, or paint lifted in a line rather than chipped in a patch. Rust in a line is a crack until you prove otherwise. The joints that matter most are the head tube junctions, the bottom bracket shell, and where the stays meet the dropouts.

For repairs, look for weld beads that do not match the rest of the frame in size or colour, added plates, filler under paint, and paint that is newer in one area than everywhere else. A brazed or re-welded joint often looks smoother and shinier than the factory welds around it.

Then sight the frame from behind for alignment and put a wheel in to see whether it sits centred. A repair is not automatically a dealbreaker, but it is always something a seller should tell you about.

Q096What should I check before buying a used BMX frame?

Six things, in this order. Welds, for cracks. Dropouts, for spreading, filing and bending. Alignment, by sighting down the frame and by dropping a wheel in to see if it sits centred. The head tube area, for ripples or ring cracks that mean a front impact. Threads and the bottom bracket shell, for stripping or a hammered bore. Then rust, distinguishing surface rust on the outside from bubbling that means rust from the inside.

After the condition checks, get the fitment facts: bottom bracket shell size, head tube and headset type, seat post diameter, rear dropout slot width, and whether it has brake mounts. Those decide what you can build with it.

Finally, measure the top tube yourself rather than trusting the listing, and note the serial number.
