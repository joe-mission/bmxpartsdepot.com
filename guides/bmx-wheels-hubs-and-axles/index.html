Wheels, Hubs and Axles

The rear hub is the part of a BMX bike with the most ways to be incompatible, and the most confidently repeated wrong numbers online. This guide separates the three hub types, then works through axle diameter and hub spacing, which are two different measurements people mix up constantly. Every figure carries its source, and where the honest answer is that nobody publishes one, it says that instead.

Old SchoolMid SchoolModern

Quick Answer

Spin the wheel backwards with the bike off the ground and watch the cranks. If the cranks stay still and you hear a ratchet, it is a cassette. If the cranks stay still and it is nearly silent, it is a freecoaster. If the cranks turn backwards with the wheel, the chain is dragging or the hub is seized.

Then look at the cog. A cassette or freecoaster has a small splined driver threaded into the hub body. A freewheel is a separate cluster screwed onto the outside of a plain threaded hub, and it comes off as one unit.

Telling the three rear hub types apart

Three designs have been used to let a BMX bike coast. They are not interchangeable, they do not share drivers, and knowing which you have is the first question in every rear wheel purchase.

Look at where the ratchet lives. On a cassette hub the ratchet mechanism is inside the hub shell and the cog sits on a small removable driver. On a freewheel hub the ratchet is inside the cog cluster itself, and the hub is a plain threaded shell that does nothing clever. On a freecoaster the mechanism is inside the hub and there is a clutch rather than a simple ratchet.

Look at the cog size. A modern cassette or freecoaster driver carries a very small cog, small enough that it looks wrong to anyone used to older bikes. A freewheel cog is much larger, because a threaded freewheel has to be big enough to screw over the hub shell threads.

Spin it backwards. Cassette hubs click loudly. Freecoasters are close to silent and let the bike roll backwards without the cranks turning at all. That last part is what a freecoaster is for.

Try to unscrew the cog. A freewheel will come off with a freewheel removal tool as one unit and leave threads behind on the hub. A cassette driver will not.

Cassette hub

Quick Answer

A cassette hub carries its ratchet inside the hub shell and drives through a small removable splined driver. It is the standard modern BMX rear hub, and the driver is a wear part you can replace without replacing the hub.

The design that took over. Putting the mechanism inside the hub means the cog can be tiny, and a tiny cog is what makes a small front sprocket possible. Everything about modern BMX gearing follows from that.

The driver threads or splines into the hub body and can be removed. That matters twice over: it is the part that wears, and it is the part that sets your gearing. A worn driver in an otherwise good hub is a cheap fix, which is why a used cassette hub with a rough driver is still worth buying if the shell and bearings are sound.

Cassette hubs are made in both right hand drive and left hand drive, and the two are not the same hub flipped around. Drivers are threaded, and the thread direction has to suit the side it is on, so a right hand drive driver will not simply serve on the left.

What to check on a used cassette hub

Grab the rim at the top and rock it side to side with the wheel in the frame. Any knock is bearing play or a loose axle, and you want to know which before you buy. Spin it and listen for grinding rather than a clean ratchet. Then take hold of the cog and try to turn it backwards slowly: a healthy ratchet engages crisply, a tired one slips or feels notchy. Check the driver splines and the cog teeth for hooking, and look at the flanges for cracks radiating from the spoke holes, which is where hubs fail.

Freecoaster

Quick Answer

A freecoaster has an internal clutch that lets the bike roll backwards without the cranks turning. That is the only functional difference that matters, and it is why riders who do fakie and backwards tricks run them.

On a cassette hub, rolling backwards drags the cranks backwards with you. On a freecoaster it does not. The clutch disengages when you roll back and re-engages when you pedal forwards.

The tradeoff is slack. There is a gap between starting to pedal and the hub picking up drive, and that gap is adjustable on most freecoasters by changing a spacer or a washer inside. Less slack means quicker engagement and a more direct feel. More slack means the hub is less likely to catch you out mid-trick. Riders have strong opinions and no two set them the same.

Freecoasters are mechanically busier than cassettes. There are more parts, they need servicing more often, and they are less tolerant of neglect. A freecoaster is not a good choice for someone who is never going to open it up. That is a real drawback and worth saying plainly rather than treating the hub as simply the better option.

For a used buyer the extra checks are: does it actually coast backwards freely, does it engage when you pedal, how much slack is there, and does the clutch feel smooth or does it clunk in. A freecoaster that does not engage reliably is a rebuild, not a bargain.

Nobody publishes this, so here is a reference point. Slack is how far the cranks turn before the hub picks up drive, and it is the single thing riders argue about with freecoasters. Measured against a clock face, 30 degrees is one hour of travel and 45 degrees is an hour and a half.

Both of these are within the normal range and neither is faulty. What you are checking on a used freecoaster is that the slack is consistent and the pickup is clean, not that it matches a number.

Freewheel

Quick Answer

A freewheel is a self-contained ratcheting cog cluster that screws onto a threaded hub shell. The hub itself has no mechanism. It is the older arrangement and it is what you will meet on old school and most department store bikes.

The whole ratchet is inside the freewheel body. That has one genuine advantage, which is simplicity, and two disadvantages that ended it. First, the freewheel has to be big enough to thread over the hub shell, so there is a hard floor on how few teeth it can have. Second, all the drive load is carried through that thread, right at the end of the axle, which is a bad place to be loading a BMX bike.

That second point is the practical one. Freewheel hubs bend axles. The drive load sits outboard of the bearing rather than inboard, and the axle takes it. This is the main reason cassette hubs replaced freewheels on anything ridden hard.

A freewheel unscrews with a freewheel removal tool that matches its splines or notches, and the tool is specific to the freewheel type. Freewheels tighten themselves under pedalling, so an old one that has never been off can be genuinely difficult to remove.

You cannot fit a freewheel to a cassette hub or a cassette driver to a freewheel hub. Different hubs entirely. If a bike has a freewheel and you want cassette gearing, you are buying a rear wheel.

There are two freewheel threads, not one, and that catches people out. Anything 16 tooth and up uses 1.375 inch x 24 TPI. Drop to 13, 14 or 15 tooth and the freewheel switches to an M30 x 1.0 metric thread, because there is not enough material left at that diameter for the larger thread. A small freewheel will not screw onto a standard hub and vice versa.

The larger thread is not a BMX thread at all. 1.375 inch x 24 TPI is the general bicycle freewheel standard, so a 16 tooth and up BMX freewheel and an ordinary single-speed bicycle freewheel share the same interface. If you are keeping an old freewheel hub alive, the parts pool is wider than the BMX shelf.

13 tooth is the floor, and it is a mechanical one rather than a market one. Below that there is no room left around the axle, which is precisely why cassette hubs exist: moving the ratchet into the hub shell is what allowed 9 and 8 tooth drivers.

Drivers and cog sizes

Quick Answer

The driver is the removable splined body on a cassette or freecoaster hub that carries the cog. Driver splines are brand-specific and mostly do not interchange, so a replacement driver is bought for the hub it is going into, by brand and model.

The driver does two jobs. It is the wear part that takes the drive load, and its tooth count is one half of your gear ratio. Change the driver and you change the gearing without touching the sprocket.

Cog size on the driver is the thing that separates a modern setup from an older one. Small drivers came in alongside small front sprockets, and the two go together: you cannot run a very small sprocket with a large driver without ending up in a gear nobody wants to pedal. That pairing is the micro drive idea, and it is covered properly in the drivetrain guide.

Drivers do not swap between brands. The spline pattern, spline count and driver diameter are design choices each manufacturer made independently. Some brands share a pattern by accident or by licensing, and there is no general rule you can rely on. Buy the driver for the hub you own.

To work out the size of the driver already on your bike, count the teeth. It is a small number and it takes ten seconds. Reading it off the driver body is unreliable because the marking, if there is one, may be the hub model rather than the tooth count.

Drivers do not interchange between brands, with one exception worth knowing

This is the opposite answer to the pivotal seat question on the seats page, and it matters more.

Aftermarket drivers are proprietary per brand and often per hub model. A Profile driver does not fit an Odyssey Hazard, and neither fits a BSD. The spline and pawl or clutch interface is specific to the hub it was designed for, and no manufacturer publishes the spline geometry. So the answer here is two findings at once: there is no cross-brand standard, and there is no published specification either, which means you cannot even check compatibility on paper. You match the driver to the hub by name.

The exception is entry-level complete bikes. Hubs on many completes come from shared factories, Joytech and Novatec among them, and those generic hubs take generic replacement drivers that do cross between bike brands. So a driver for a Salt, Mission or Rant entry-level hub may well be the same part, because the hub is the same part with different branding.

That is a genuinely useful split for buying used: on an aftermarket hub, source by hub name. On a complete bike hub, the generic driver is probably right. <span class="src src-confirmed">Confirmed</span>

One correction to a common assumption, including one this guide previously made. The driver does not thread into the hub shell. The driver body drops in. What threads into the shell is the internal ratchet ring, and manufacturers do not publish its thread diameter or pitch either.

Checked twice now across Profile, Odyssey, BSD and Eclat, technical archives and overhaul guides both. Neither the driver spline geometry nor the ratchet ring thread is published by anyone. The interfaces are proprietary and undocumented, which is why matching by hub name is not laziness, it is the only method available. <span class="src src-conflict">Unpublished</span>

Axle diameter, male axles and female axles

Quick Answer

Modern freestyle BMX rear hubs use a 14mm axle. Race hubs use smaller axles, including 10mm. Separately, 3/8 inch and 10mm are not the same size: 3/8 inch is 9.525mm, so a 10mm axle is about half a millimetre larger.

Axle diameter is the outside diameter of the axle itself. It decides which dropout slots the wheel will drop into, which pegs will fit, and what hardware you need.

The 14mm axle is the freestyle standard and the reason is straightforward: a bigger axle bends less, and BMX bikes get landed on. Race hubs run smaller and lighter axles because race bikes do not take peg loads or drop-in impacts, and weight matters more.

3/8 inch and 10mm are different sizes

This is the confusion worth spending a paragraph on. 3/8 inch is 0.375 inch, which is 9.525mm. A 10mm axle is 10.0mm. They are about 0.475mm apart, which is close enough that sellers use the terms interchangeably in listings and far enough apart that the hardware is not the same. No source we found endorses treating them as one size.

Neither is interchangeable with 14mm.

If a hub or axle is not marked, the honest listing says which one it measures with calipers, or says it was not measured. Guessing is how a buyer ends up with axle nuts that will not start.

Male and female axles

A male axle is threaded on the outside and takes nuts that thread onto the axle. A female axle is hollow and internally threaded, and takes bolts that thread into it. Both are common and neither is better in general.

The difference matters for two reasons. Hardware is not shared: nuts for a male axle are useless on a female axle and vice versa. And peg fitment differs, because a female axle setup runs a bolt through the peg into the axle, which changes how much thread engagement you get and which pegs and spacers work.

Female axles usually sit flush or nearly flush with the dropout, which is tidier and less likely to catch on things. Male axles protrude, which some riders prefer for peg mounting. What you cannot do is decide on looks and then assume the hardware will follow.

Those are manufacturing tolerance and light wear, not a different size, and none would stop the axle going into the dropout it was made for.

The number that matters is which size you are looking at, not the second decimal place. A reading anywhere near 9.5 is a 3/8 inch axle and a reading near 14 is a 14mm axle, and nothing in between exists as a BMX standard. If a listing says 10mm and the part measures 9.5, the listing is describing a 3/8 inch axle by the wrong name, which is the confusion this section exists to clear up.

Hub spacing and over locknut dimension

Quick Answer

Over locknut dimension is the width of the hub measured across the outer faces of its locknuts, and it has nothing to do with axle diameter. BMX rear dropout spacing is 110mm nominal, though a source from Odyssey describes a real frame ending up at 108mm, so expect measured frames to come in slightly under.

This is the measurement people confuse with axle diameter, and the two answer completely different questions. Axle diameter asks whether the axle drops into the slot. Over locknut dimension asks whether the hub fits between the dropouts at all.

Measure it across the outermost faces of the locknuts, not across the flanges and not including the axle sticking out past them. On the frame side, measure between the inner faces of the dropouts with the wheel out.

Hub end caps and dish

Hub end caps and spacers set the over locknut dimension, and on some hubs they can be changed. That is how one hub gets sold to suit more than one spacing, and it is also how a hub arrives at your door set up for something other than what you have. Count and keep every spacer that comes off a used wheel.

Wheel dish is how centred the rim sits between the locknuts. A wheel built for one frame can sit off-centre in another, and a wheel that has been rebuilt badly can be off-centre in every frame. Drop the wheel into the frame and look at the gap either side of the tyre. If it is uneven, swap in a wheel you trust to find out whether it is the wheel or the frame.

Rims, wall construction and bead seat diameter

Quick Answer

Rim wall construction is the single biggest strength difference between two used rims. A single wall rim has one layer of material under the spoke nipples. A double wall rim has a second inner wall, which is stronger and heavier. Triple wall adds more again.

Look into the rim through the valve hole or at a spoke nipple. On a single wall rim you can see straight through to the tyre bed and the nipples sit in that same surface. On a double wall rim there is a closed inner box section, and the nipples pass through a hole in the outer wall into a cavity.

Single wall rims dent and go out of true easily under real riding. They are fine on a bike that is ridden gently and they are what most complete bikes at the cheap end come with. Double wall is the practical minimum for anything that gets jumped, and it is what almost all BMX rims sold on their own are. Triple wall exists, is heavier again, and shows up on bikes that get very hard use.

A dented rim is worth noticing early. Look along the braking surface, spin the wheel and watch the rim against the brake pad or a fixed point, and look at the sidewall for flat spots and for cracks radiating from spoke holes. Cracks at the spoke holes are the end of the rim.

ISO bead seat diameter

ISO bead seat diameter is the actual measured diameter of the rim where the tyre bead sits, and it is the only reliable way to know a tyre and rim will go together. Nominal sizes like "20 inch" are trade names rather than measurements, and there is history in bicycles of two sizes sharing a nominal name and not fitting each other at all.

In practice, BMX is much better behaved than the rest of cycling here, because the standard 20 inch BMX wheel is genuinely standard. The reason to know the term is for everything that is not that: cruisers, smaller kids' bikes, and anything unusual that comes through in a job lot. The ISO number is marked on most tyres as a pair of figures, the width and the bead seat diameter.

ISO 406 is the number that matters, and it is a standard rather than a convention, which makes it one of the few figures on this site you can rely on absolutely. A "20 inch" BMX wheel is a 406mm bead seat with a tyre on top, which is also why it rolls about 19.5 inches rather than 20, as covered on the drivetrain guide.

On valves, a Presta tube will physically pass through an 8.5mm Schrader drilling but should not be run bare in one. Use a grommet. The hole is nearly 2mm oversize for the stem, so the valve gets pushed sideways every time the tyre moves on the rim, and that is how a stem tears at the base.

Spoke count and lacing

Quick Answer

Count the spokes at the hub flange and double it, or count the nipples around the rim. BMX wheels are commonly 36 spoke, with 48 spoke wheels found on older and heavy-duty builds. Three cross is the normal BMX lacing pattern.

Spoke count is easy to establish and easy to get wrong by miscounting. The reliable method is to count nipples around one side of the rim and multiply, or count spokes leaving one flange and multiply by two. A 48 spoke wheel looks visibly busier than a 36 and the flanges are drilled with noticeably more holes.

More spokes spread the load over more points, which is stronger, and add weight and build time. 48 spoke wheels were the heavy-duty answer for a long time and turn up on old school and mid school bikes constantly. Modern rims and hubs are strong enough that 36 spoke does the job for most riders, which is why it is the default now.

Three cross means each spoke crosses three others between hub and rim. It is what almost every BMX wheel is built with. Lower cross counts exist and change the spoke length required, which is the practical consequence: lacing pattern and spoke length are the same question asked twice.

Spokes on BMX wheels are J-bend, meaning the head has a bent elbow that hooks through the flange. That is worth knowing because straight-pull spokes exist elsewhere in cycling and are not interchangeable.

Working out spoke length for a rebuild

You need four numbers to calculate spoke length: hub flange diameter, hub centre to flange distance on each side, rim effective diameter, and the lacing pattern. Then a spoke length calculator does the arithmetic. There is no shortcut and there is no universal BMX spoke length, because hub flange dimensions vary between hubs.

The honest practical route on a used wheel is to measure a spoke that is already in it, if the wheel is currently built and correct. That gives you the answer without touching a calculator, and it is why it is worth measuring before you strip a wheel rather than after.

Hub guards, drive side and left hand drive

Quick Answer

A hub guard is a disc that protects the hub flange and spokes during grinds, and it has to match the hub, the axle diameter and the side it is going on. Left hand drive means the sprocket, chain and driver are on the left of the bike rather than the conventional right.

Hub guards are not generic. They fit a specific hub design, or a specific family of hub designs, and they come in drive side and non drive side versions because the two sides of a rear hub are shaped differently. They also have to suit the axle diameter, and on female axle setups the bolt arrangement changes what works.

The reason riders want them is simple: a grind that lands on the hub flange rather than the peg will chew spokes and flange metal. A guard turns that into a replaceable part.

Left hand drive and right hand drive

Right hand drive is the conventional arrangement, with the sprocket, chain and driver on the right. Left hand drive puts all of it on the left instead.

Riders run left hand drive to keep the drivetrain out of the way of the side they grind on, or because they prefer to grind on the right and would rather the chain was not there. The change is not cosmetic and it is not a flip.

A left hand drive setup needs a left hand drive rear hub, or a hub that can be converted with the correct parts, and a left hand drive driver, because the driver threads into the hub and thread direction has to suit the side. It also usually needs the front sprocket moved to the other side of the cranks, which brings chainline and sprocket mounting into it.

The practical warning for a used buyer: a left hand drive wheel is not a right hand drive wheel turned around. If a listing offers a wheel without saying which it is, ask, because the wheel will physically go in the frame either way and only drive correctly one way.

Male axles

Quick Answer

A male axle is a threaded shaft that runs through the hub and sticks out past the dropouts on both sides, secured with a nut on each end. If you can see external threads outside the dropout, it is a male axle.

That visible thread is the whole identification, and it is also the weakness. The exposed threads are the first thing to go on a bike that gets ridden on ledges, because they are the outermost part of the axle and they take the hit. Once they are flattened, the nut will not start, and you are into a die or a new axle.

Male axles are simpler, cheaper and easier to replace, and on a bike that never grinds they are perfectly good. Check the threads on both sides before buying, and check that a nut actually runs down them by hand rather than trusting how they look.

Female axles

Quick Answer

A female axle is hollow and threaded on the inside, with a bolt screwing into each end and end caps sitting flush against the dropout. There are no threads outside the dropout to damage. If you see a bolt head rather than a nut, it is a female axle.

Street riders prefer female for one specific reason: a smooth bolt head slides over a ledge, where an exposed male thread catches and gets destroyed. That is the entire argument and it is a good one for anyone who grinds.

The corresponding weakness is that the damage moves inside where you cannot see it. A rounded bolt head or a stripped internal thread means removing the bolt is a problem, and an axle with a snapped bolt in it is a bigger job than a flattened male thread. Check that both bolts come out and go back in before buying a used female hub, rather than only looking at the outside.

Thread pitch on 3/8 inch hardware is not consistent. 26 TPI is the commonly quoted standard for BMX axle nuts, and female bolts turn up sold as 24 TPI as well. Match the hardware to the axle you actually have rather than ordering on the nominal size alone.

Hub guards

Quick Answer

A hub guard is a disc that sits over the hub against the dropout to take grind damage instead of the spokes and flange. Drive side and non-drive side guards are different parts, and they are not interchangeable.

The non-drive side guard is a plain disc. It protects the flange and the spoke heads on that side. The drive side guard has to clear the driver and the chain, so it is cut differently, and a non-drive guard fitted on the drive side will foul the chain.

Guards are also hub specific more often than people expect, because they locate on the hub body or the axle in a way that depends on the hub design. Assume a guard fits the hub it came off unless you can confirm otherwise.

Checking a used one is quick. On plastic guards, look at the retention tabs, which are the press fit ears that hold it on, because those crack and once cracked the guard spins or falls off. On aluminium or steel guards, check the threads and look for deep gouges on the inner face that would bind against the hub body. A guard that has done its job is scarred on the outside, which is fine. One that is damaged where it mounts is not.

Right hand drive and left hand drive

Quick Answer

Right hand drive (RHD) puts the sprocket, chain and driver on the right of the bike, which is the standard arrangement. Left hand drive (LHD) puts them on the left. Converting between them is not a matter of turning parts around.

A conversion needs two things at minimum: an LHD rear hub, because the driver threads on and thread direction has to suit the side it is on, and LHD cranks, because the sprocket mounts to a boss or spline on the correct arm. Neither an RHD hub nor an RHD crankset can be flipped to do the job.

Riders run LHD to suit which foot they grind with, so it is a preference rather than an upgrade. For a used buyer the thing to be careful about is a partial conversion. A bike advertised as LHD with only one of the two parts changed is not an LHD bike, and the other half is a specific purchase rather than something you have lying around. Check the hub and the cranks separately before believing the listing.

Questions

Q028How do I tell if a BMX rear hub is a cassette, freecoaster, or freewheel?

Lift the back wheel and spin it backwards. A cassette clicks loudly and the cranks stay still. A freecoaster is close to silent and the cranks stay still. Neither drags the cranks backwards.

Then look at the cog. A cassette and a freecoaster both drive through a small splined driver threaded into the hub, and the cog is small. A freewheel is a much larger cluster screwed onto the outside of a plain threaded hub, and it unscrews as a single unit with the right tool.

The quickest tell between a cassette and a freecoaster is rolling the bike backwards on the ground. On a cassette the cranks turn backwards with you. On a freecoaster they do not, which is the entire reason freecoasters exist.

Q024What is the difference between a 14mm and a 3/8 inch BMX rear axle?

Diameter, and what they are used for. A 14mm axle is 14.0mm and is the modern freestyle standard, chosen because a thicker axle bends less under landings and peg loads. A 3/8 inch axle is 9.525mm, so it is considerably thinner, and it is found on older bikes, on lighter builds and on front hubs.

They are not interchangeable in either direction. Different dropout slot width, different axle nuts, different pegs.

There is a third size in this conversation. A 10mm axle is 10.0mm, which is not the same as 3/8 inch even though the two get used interchangeably in listings. The difference is about half a millimetre, which is enough that the hardware does not swap.

Q025Will a 14mm axle fit my frame dropouts?

Only if the dropout slot is wide enough, and the way to find out is to measure the slot. A 14mm axle needs a slot at least 14mm wide, and a frame built around a smaller axle will have a narrower one.

People do file dropout slots open to fit a bigger axle. We would not recommend it and we would not sell a frame that has had it done without saying so. The slot is the highest-loaded part of the dropout and filing removes material from exactly the wrong place, as well as leaving an axle sitting in an uneven slot.

Note that this is a different question from whether the hub fits between the dropouts. That is hub spacing, measured across the locknuts, and BMX rear spacing is 110mm nominal with real frames often measuring slightly under. A 14mm axle in a 110mm frame is entirely normal.

Q026What is the difference between a male and a female BMX axle?

A male axle is threaded on the outside and takes nuts that screw onto it. A female axle is hollow and threaded on the inside, and takes bolts that screw into it.

The hardware does not swap. Nuts for a male axle are no use on a female axle, and the two need different pegs and different spacers because the mounting arrangement is different.

Female axles generally sit flush or close to flush with the dropout, which is tidier. Male axles protrude, and some riders prefer that for peg mounting. Neither is stronger in general; what matters is that the hardware matches and there is enough thread engagement.

Q027What is the difference between a single wall and a double wall BMX rim?

How many layers of material are under the spoke nipples. A single wall rim has one, so you can see straight through the spoke hole into the tyre bed. A double wall rim has a second inner wall forming a closed box section, and the nipple passes through the outer wall into that cavity.

Double wall is substantially stronger and somewhat heavier. It resists denting and it holds true far better under impact. Single wall rims are what cheap complete bikes come with and they go out of shape quickly if the bike is actually jumped.

For anything ridden hard, double wall is the practical minimum. Triple wall exists and is heavier again.

Q023How do I tell a 36 spoke wheel from a 48 spoke wheel?

Count the nipples around one side of the rim and multiply by two, or count the spokes leaving one hub flange and multiply by two. Counting the whole wheel in one go is where people go wrong.

You can also tell by eye once you have seen both. A 48 spoke wheel looks visibly denser and the hub flanges are drilled with noticeably more holes.

48 spoke wheels are the older heavy-duty standard and turn up constantly on old school and mid school bikes. 36 spoke is the modern default because rims and hubs are strong enough now that the extra spokes are not needed for most riders.

Q022What is the real rim diameter of a 20 inch BMX wheel?

"20 inch" is a trade name rather than a measurement, and the number that actually matters is the ISO bead seat diameter, which is the measured diameter of the rim where the tyre bead sits. That figure is printed on most tyres as part of a pair, width and bead seat diameter.

The figure is ISO 406mm, sourced to ISO 5775 and ETRTO. A 20 inch BMX wheel is a 406mm bead seat with a tyre on top, which is also why it rolls closer to 19.5 inches than to 20. Because it is a standard rather than a convention, it is one of the few figures on this site you can rely on absolutely, and 20 inch BMX tyres and rims go together reliably.

The trap is ISO 451, used on some narrow race wheels and some older ones. 406 and 451 do not interchange in either direction, so if a tyre will not seat, read the number on the sidewall before blaming the tyre. Full detail in rims, wall construction and bead seat diameter.

Where the ISO number earns its keep is on everything that is not a standard 20 inch BMX wheel: cruisers, smaller kids' bikes, and old wheels of uncertain origin.

Q036How do I tell what size driver is on my BMX hub?

Count the teeth. It is a small number and it takes seconds, and it is more reliable than any marking on the driver body, which is often the hub model rather than the tooth count.

You will need that number for two things: working out your gear ratio, since the driver is one half of it, and buying a replacement, since drivers are sold by tooth count.

Bear in mind that the driver you can buy is constrained by the hub. Driver splines are brand-specific and mostly do not interchange, so you buy a driver for your hub by brand and model, then choose the tooth count within what that hub offers.

Q099How do I check a used BMX hub for play or bad bearings?

With the wheel in the frame, hold the rim at the top and rock it side to side. Any knock you can feel is play, and it is either loose bearings, a worn bearing, or a loose axle. Nip the axle up and try again to work out which.

Then take the wheel out, hold the axle in your fingers and turn it. It should turn smoothly with light drag. Grinding, notchiness or a gritty feel means the bearings are done. A bearing that feels rough in one spot on every rotation is pitted.

On a cassette or freecoaster, also check the mechanism. Turn the cog backwards slowly and feel for crisp engagement rather than slipping. On a freecoaster, roll it backwards and then pedal forwards to check it picks up drive reliably.

Sealed cartridge bearings are usually replaceable and a hub with a bad bearing and a good shell is worth having. A cracked flange is not fixable and the hub is scrap.
