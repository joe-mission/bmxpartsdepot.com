Headsets, Forks and Stems

The front end is where the most confidently wrong numbers on the internet live. This guide covers how each part of a BMX front end works and what determines fit, and it holds back the diameters and offsets until they have been measured or sourced rather than repeating figures that do not hold up.

Old SchoolMid SchoolModern

Quick Answer

The head tube decides the headset, and the fork steerer decides the stem. Look inside your head tube: bearings sitting in a machined seat with no cups means an integrated headset, cups pressed into the tube means a zero stack or traditional pressed headset, and a threaded ring at the top with a locknut means an old threaded headset.

Then look at the steerer sticking out of it. Threads on the outside of the steerer means a quill stem. A smooth steerer with a stem clamped around it means threadless. Those two systems share nothing.

Headset standards, and why the head tube decides

Quick Answer

A headset is the pair of bearings that lets the fork rotate in the frame. The head tube design dictates which headset fits, and you cannot change a frame from one type to another. Identify the frame first, then buy the headset, then buy the fork and stem to suit.

There are three questions to answer about any front end, in this order.

How do the bearings sit in the head tube? Directly in a machined seat, in cups pressed into the tube, or in cups that sit mostly outside it. That is the headset standard, and it is a property of the frame.

Is the steerer threaded or smooth? That is the fork, and it decides the stem type.

What diameter is the steerer? That is what the headset and stem have to match.

Those three are independent of each other in principle and heavily correlated in practice, because bikes came as sets. A frame with a threaded headset almost always came with a threaded fork and a quill stem. A modern integrated frame almost always came with a threadless fork and a clamp stem. But on used parts the assumptions break, and you should check all three rather than assume the third from the first.

Integrated headsets

Quick Answer

An integrated headset has the bearing seat machined directly into the head tube, so the bearings drop straight in with no cups at all. It is the modern BMX standard, and a frame cut for integrated cannot take a pressed cup headset.

The design is the logical end point of the same thinking that produced the Mid bottom bracket. If the frame can be machined accurately enough, you do not need a cup to hold the bearing, and removing the cup removes weight, removes a part that can creak, and lets the whole assembly sit lower.

What you see when you look into the head tube of an integrated frame is a smooth angled seat, and a cartridge bearing sitting in it with nothing between the bearing and the frame. Above the bearing sits a compression ring and a dust cover, and that is the entire top of the headset.

Two things follow.

You cannot convert a frame. The seat is machined into the tube at manufacture. A frame that is cut for integrated bearings is cut for integrated bearings. There is no adapter that turns it into a pressed cup frame, and there is no reason to want one.

The frame is the wear part. On a pressed cup headset, a bearing that has been run dry and beaten up chews the cup, and you press a new cup in. On an integrated headset there is no cup, so a bearing that has been hammering away in a dry seat is beating on the frame itself. That is why an integrated head tube is worth inspecting closely on a used frame: look for a bright polished ring or a stepped groove where the bearing has been sitting.

Zero stack and pressed cup headsets

Quick Answer

A zero stack headset (semi-integrated) uses cups pressed into the head tube with the bearings sitting inside the tube, so almost nothing protrudes. A traditional pressed headset uses cups that sit mostly outside the tube, raising the whole front end.

Both are pressed cup systems and both are found on used BMX frames, so the distinction matters.

On a traditional pressed headset, the cup is a top-hat shape: a short section presses into the head tube and a flange sits proudly on the face of the tube, with the bearing sitting in the part outside. Look at the frame from the side and you see a visible ring of headset above and below the head tube. This is what most older frames use.

On a zero stack, the cup presses further in and the bearing lives inside the head tube bore. From the side, very little shows. The head tube bore has to be larger to swallow the cup, which is why a zero stack frame and a traditional frame are not interchangeable even though both are "pressed cup".

The practical consequences on a used bike are height and identification. A traditional pressed headset adds more height above the head tube than a zero stack does, which raises the bars and changes how the bike feels before you have touched a single component. And when buying a headset for a used frame, "it presses in" is not enough information: you need to know which pressed standard the frame is bored for.

The good news for a buyer is that a pressed cup frame is repairable in a way an integrated frame is not. Cups take the wear, and cups can be pressed out and replaced.

Threaded headsets and quill stems

Quick Answer

A threaded headset has a top race that screws onto a threaded fork steerer, locked by a locknut. It goes with a quill stem, which slides down inside the hollow steerer and is locked by an expander bolt pulling a wedge up inside it. Threaded and threadless share no parts.

The threaded system was how every bike worked for a long time and it is what you will find on old school BMX. It is worth understanding properly because a lot of used old school parts are sold by people who have only ever seen threadless.

How the headset works. The lower race sits on the fork crown. The bearings run in cups in the head tube. The top race threads onto the steerer and sets the bearing preload, and a locknut is tightened down against it to stop the adjustment moving. Between the two there is usually a keyed washer with a tab that locates in a groove down the steerer, which is what stops the top race turning when you tighten the locknut.

How the stem works. The steerer sticks up through the headset and is hollow. The quill (the long shaft of the stem) drops down inside it. An expander bolt runs the length of the quill and threads into a wedge at the bottom. Tightening the bolt pulls the wedge up into the tapered end of the quill, spreading it against the inside of the steerer. That is the entire mechanism, and it is why a quill stem can be set at any height within its range simply by loosening one bolt.

The system has real advantages: height adjustment is trivial, and there is only one bolt to deal with. It also has failure modes that a threadless setup does not.

The expander bolt seizes. This is the classic old school problem. The bolt is steel, sitting inside a steel steerer, often for decades, frequently with water in it. On a used bike, an expander bolt that will not turn is common and a stem that will not come out is common.

The wedge drops. Undo the expander bolt too far and the wedge falls off the bottom of the bolt and drops into the fork steerer, where it rattles and is a real nuisance to retrieve.

Overtightening splits the quill. The tapered end is thin. Riders who have had a stem slip tend to respond by tightening harder, which spreads the quill further than it was meant to go.

Minimum insertion is not optional. A quill stem has a maximum height line marked on it. Above that line there is not enough quill inside the steerer to clamp safely, and the failure mode is the stem coming out of the fork. A quill stem with the maximum line above the top of the headset does not get ridden and does not get sold like that.

Threadless headsets and compression

Quick Answer

A threadless headset runs on a smooth steerer, and the stem clamps around the outside of it. Preload comes from a compression system: a cap and bolt pull down on the top of the steerer against something anchored inside it, which squeezes the whole stack together, and then the stem's pinch bolts lock everything in place.

The threadless idea, generically known as an Aheadset after the trade name that popularised it, changes what the parts do.

On a threaded setup, the top race does the preload and the stem is an independent part hanging inside the steerer. On a threadless setup, the stem is a structural part of the headset assembly. The stack from bottom to top is: lower bearing, head tube, upper bearing, compression ring, dust cover, any spacers, then the stem clamped around the steerer. The stem is what holds the stack together once it is set.

Preload is set separately from clamping, and the order matters enormously.

The compression system pulls the stack down. A top cap sits on the stem and a bolt runs down through it into something gripping the inside of the steerer, either a star nut hammered into the tube or, more usually on BMX, an expanding compression plug or wedge. Tightening the cap bolt pulls the top of the stack downward against the fork crown, which takes all the slack out of the bearings.

The stem pinch bolts then clamp the stem to the steerer and lock that setting in.

If you tighten the pinch bolts first and then the cap bolt, nothing happens, because the stem is already locked and cannot move down. This is the single most common front end setup mistake, and the symptom is a headset that will not stop knocking no matter how hard you tighten the cap. Loosen the pinch bolts, set preload with the cap, then tighten the pinch bolts.

Two other points that matter on a used bike.

The cap bolt is not a structural fastener. It sets preload and that is all. Once the pinch bolts are tight, the cap bolt could fall out and the stem would still be clamped to the steerer. It is not carrying your weight.

BMX often uses a compression plug rather than a star nut, because BMX steerers are frequently thicker walled and because a plug can be removed and reused where a star nut is a one-way trip. If a used fork has a star nut hammered down inside it and you want to run a plug, the star nut has to come out, which usually means driving it down and out of the bottom of the steerer.

Top load and front load stems

Quick Answer

A front load stem has a removable faceplate that clamps the bars from the front. A top load stem clamps the bars from above, usually with a cap that lifts off upward. Front load stems generally sit the bars lower and let you swap bars without taking off grips and levers. Top load stems generally sit the bars higher.

This is the choice riders actually make on a build, and it is worth being clear about what it does.

Bar height. The clamp on a top load stem sits above the steerer clamp, so the bars end up higher. On a front load stem the bar clamp sits ahead of the steerer clamp at roughly the same height, so the bars end up lower. That is a real difference in how the bike feels, and it stacks with the bar rise you have chosen and with how many headset spacers are under the stem. Riders who want their bars high often run a top load with a taller bar. Riders who want a lower front end run a front load.

Ease of bar swapping. A front load faceplate comes off with its bolts and the bars lift straight out forward. Grips, levers and the gyro upper cables can all stay where they are. A top load usually means sliding the bars out sideways through the clamp, which means stripping one end of the cockpit. If you change bars often, front load saves you time every single time.

Rigidity and looks. Riders argue about which is stiffer. What is genuinely true is that a front load has a joint (the faceplate) that a top load may not, and that faceplate has to be tightened evenly. Tighten a faceplate in a criss-cross pattern with an even gap top and bottom, not one bolt at a time until it stops. An uneven faceplate gap is a stress riser on the bars.

There is a third arrangement worth naming: some stems clamp the bars in a way that is neither cleanly top nor front load, and old school stems in particular vary a lot. Judge those by looking rather than by the category name.

The oversize bar clamp question is separate and it is a bar question rather than a stem question. Stems and bars have to agree on clamp diameter exactly, and more than one diameter exists. Do not assume, and do not force a bar into a clamp that seems close.

Steerer tube length and stack

Quick Answer

Steerer length decides how much stem and how many spacers you can run. Measure it from the top of the fork crown race seat to the top of the steerer, and compare that against your head tube length plus your headset stack plus your stem.

A fork that is too short for a frame simply will not work: the stem clamp will hang off the top of the steerer with not enough tube inside it. A fork that is too long is normally fine, because the excess goes into spacers under the stem, and on a threadless setup a steerer can be cut down.

The measurement that actually matters is not the total length of the steerer but how much of it is left above the head tube once the headset is in. That is why the sum is worth doing rather than guessed:

Head tube length plus headset stack height (the amount the headset adds above and below the head tube, which differs a lot between integrated, zero stack and traditional pressed) gives you the height at which the steerer emerges. Everything above that is available for spacers and the stem clamp.

On a used fork, the steerer has usually already been cut for whatever frame it was on. A cut steerer cannot be uncut. So when buying a used fork, measure the steerer, work out what it will do in your frame, and do not rely on a listing that says "fits any BMX frame".

On a threaded fork, there is a second constraint: the threaded section has a finite length, and the top race and locknut have to land inside it. A threaded steerer that is long enough physically but has run out of thread is no more usable than one that is too short.

Fork offset

Quick Answer

Fork offset, also called rake, is how far the dropouts sit ahead of the steerer tube centreline. Together with head tube angle it sets the trail of the bike, which is what makes the steering feel fast and twitchy or slow and stable.

This is the one front end spec that most people ignore and then feel immediately when they change it.

Imagine the line of the steerer extended down to the ground. That line hits the ground somewhere in front of the tyre contact patch, and the distance between the two points is trail. Trail is what makes a bike want to steer straight: turn the bars, and the contact patch drags behind the steering axis, generating a force that pulls the wheel back into line. More trail, more self-centring, more stability. Less trail, less self-centring, quicker steering.

Increasing fork offset moves the axle forward, which reduces trail. So a fork with more offset gives quicker, lighter steering. A fork with less offset gives more stable, heavier steering. That relationship is the whole reason offset is on a spec sheet.

The catch is that offset only means something in combination with head tube angle, and both are frame and fork properties that only interact once the parts are on the same bike. A fork that gave one bike lively steering can make another feel vague, because the head angles differ.

What you can do practically, when replacing a used fork, is measure the offset on the fork that is coming off and match it. The bike you already know is a better reference than a number off a chart.

Fork dropouts and axle sizing

Quick Answer

The dropout slot width is the width of the axle slot in the fork ends. It has to accept your front axle, and a slot narrower than the axle means the wheel does not go in. Front and rear axles on BMX are not the same size, so a rear wheel's hardware is not a guide.

On BMX forks, the dropouts are at the bottom of the legs and the slot usually opens downward or slightly rearward. Three things to look at on a used fork.

Slot width. Measure it. If a front hub's axle is larger than the slot, the options are a different hub, a different fork, or filing the dropouts, and filing dropouts on a fork is not something to do lightly.

Slot depth and the axle seating. The axle should sit right at the closed end of the slot with the wheel centred in the fork. A wheel that will not pull up to the end of the slot is either the wrong axle or has hardware in the way.

Dropout thickness and peg clearance. Front pegs bolt onto the axle outside the dropout, and how much axle protrudes past the dropout face determines whether a peg gets enough thread engagement. Thick dropouts eat axle length.

A unicrown fork, where the legs bend inward and meet the steerer directly with no separate machined crown, is the common modern construction and is also an identification marker: a fork with a separate brazed or welded crown is generally an older design. That is a dating cue rather than a fitment one, but it is useful when working out what era a mixed-up bike is from.

Headset play, wear, and checking a used front end

Quick Answer

Headset play feels like a knock through the bars when you brake or land. Hold the front brake on and rock the bike forward and back: a click or knock at the head tube, with no movement at the stem, is a loose or worn headset.

The check itself takes ten seconds and everyone should know it.

Stand beside the bike, pull the front brake on hard, and rock the bike back and forward while resting a finger across the gap between the head tube and the top of the headset. If you feel a knock and a tiny movement at that joint, you have play. If you feel a knock but the movement is somewhere else, look at the stem clamp, the hub bearings, or the brake itself before blaming the headset.

Then lift the front wheel off the ground and let the bars fall to one side. A healthy headset lets the bars swing freely and smoothly. If the bars fall and then stop in one particular position, or if turning the bars feels notchy, the bearings have indexed: the balls have hammered small dents into the races, usually from being run loose or from repeated hard landings, and they now want to sit in those dents. That is not adjustable away. It is a new headset, or on an integrated frame it may be a new bearing and a very careful look at the frame seat.

What causes it, in order of how often it turns up on used bikes:

Run loose. A headset with play does not just knock, it destroys itself, because every knock is a bearing hitting a race with the whole weight of the bike behind it. A headset ridden loose for a month is done.

Run dry. No grease, water in, corrosion in the races.

Overtightened preload. Less common, but a headset done up until the bars will not turn freely is loading the bearings continuously and will wear.

Bad installation. Cups pressed in crooked, a crown race not seated fully down, or a compression ring left out entirely. All of these show up as a headset that never feels right no matter how it is adjusted.

Alongside the headset, three things to check on any used fork.

Straightness. Sight down the fork from the front with the wheel out. The legs should be symmetric and the dropouts level with each other. Then look from the side: a fork that has taken a hard front impact bends backwards near the crown, and it is easier to see from the side than from the front.

The crown race seat. The machined step at the bottom of the steerer where the crown race sits should be clean and square. A chewed crown race seat, usually from someone levering an old race off badly, means the new race will not sit square.

Cracks at the welds. Look where the legs meet the crown or the steerer, and around the dropouts and any brake mounts. A crack in a fork is a hard no. If a fork is cracked, bent, or sketchy, it does not get sold.

Questions

Q012What size headset does a BMX bike use?

The honest answer is that you have to look at the frame, because the head tube design decides it. Bearings sitting directly in a machined angled seat with no cups means an integrated headset. Cups pressed into the tube with the bearings inside it means zero stack. Cups sitting proud of the head tube faces means a traditional pressed headset. A threaded ring and locknut on top of the steerer means an old threaded headset.

Then there is the steerer diameter, which the headset and stem both have to match. This guide does not publish the diameter figures yet, because the commonly repeated numbers in this area have not been checked against a manufacturer source and several of them do not hold up. Measure your steerer and your head tube bore, or take the old headset to the counter with you.

Q013What is the difference between an integrated and a press in BMX headset?

An integrated headset has no cups. The bearing seat is machined into the head tube itself and the cartridge bearing drops straight in. A press in headset has cups that are pressed into the head tube, and the bearings sit in the cups.

The practical difference on a used frame is what wears out. On a press in headset, the cup takes the abuse and a worn cup can be pressed out and replaced. On an integrated headset there is no cup, so a bearing that has been run loose or dry has been hammering the frame. Inspect an integrated head tube for a polished ring or a step where the bearing sits, because that damage is not fixable with a new bearing.

The two are not interchangeable in either direction. A frame is machined for one or the other at manufacture.

Q014How do I tell if my BMX headset is threaded or threadless?

Look at the top of the fork steerer where it comes out of the head tube. If you can see threads cut into the outside of the steerer, with a race screwed onto them and a locknut on top, it is a threaded headset. The stem on that bike will be a quill stem, sliding down inside the steerer with a single bolt on top.

If the steerer is smooth and a stem is clamped around the outside of it with pinch bolts, it is threadless. There will usually be a top cap with a single bolt sitting on the stem, which sets bearing preload rather than holding the stem on.

The quick version: threaded means the stem goes inside the fork, threadless means the stem goes around it.

Q015Will a threadless fork fit a frame that came with a threaded headset?

Sometimes, and it depends on the head tube rather than on what was in it before. The headset standard is a property of the frame, so a frame with pressed cups can take a threadless headset using pressed cups of the right size, and a threadless fork will then run in it.

What has to line up is the steerer diameter and the head tube bore. Older threaded setups and modern threadless setups frequently use different steerer diameters, and if they differ then the fork will not work in that frame with that headset. You will also need a threadless stem, because your existing quill stem is useless on a smooth steerer, and the bars have to suit the new stem's clamp.

Measure the head tube bore and the steerer of the fork you are considering before buying anything. This guide does not publish those diameters yet, because they have not been sourced.

Q016How do I measure BMX fork steerer tube length?

Measure from the top of the crown race seat, the machined step at the bottom of the steerer where the crown race sits, up to the top of the steerer. That is the length that has to accommodate the head tube, the headset, any spacers, and the stem clamp.

Total length on its own does not tell you whether a fork fits your frame. Do the sum: head tube length plus the headset's stack height gives you where the steerer emerges, and everything above that is available for spacers and stem. On a used fork the steerer has usually already been cut to suit somebody else's frame, and a cut steerer cannot be uncut, so measure before you buy rather than after.

Q017What does fork offset do on a BMX bike?

Offset, also called rake, is how far the dropouts sit ahead of the steerer centreline, and it changes how quickly the bike steers. More offset moves the front axle forward, which reduces trail, which reduces the self-centring effect and makes the steering feel quicker and lighter. Less offset does the opposite and makes the bike feel more stable and more planted.

Offset only means anything in combination with the frame's head tube angle, because trail depends on both. That is why the same fork can feel different on two frames. When replacing a used fork, the reliable approach is to measure the offset on the fork you are taking off and match it, rather than picking a number from a chart.

Q020How do I check a used BMX fork for bends or cracks?

Take the wheel out and look at the fork from the front. The legs should be symmetrical and the dropouts level with each other. Then look from the side, because the common damage from a hard front impact is the legs bending backwards near the crown, and that is much easier to see in profile than head on.

Next go over the welds with your eyes close to the metal and good light: where the legs meet the crown or the steerer, around the dropouts, and around any brake mounts. Look for cracked paint in a line, which often means a crack under it. Check the crown race seat at the bottom of the steerer is clean and square rather than chewed from a race being levered off. Finally check the steerer itself is straight and, on a threaded fork, that the threads are not damaged.

A fork that is cracked or bent is scrap. Forks are the one part where there is no argument about it.

Q066What is the difference between a front load and a top load BMX stem?

A front load stem clamps the bars from the front with a removable faceplate. A top load stem clamps them from above. The two effects that matter are bar height and how easily you can swap bars.

A top load puts the bar clamp above the steerer clamp, so the bars sit higher. A front load puts the clamp ahead of the steerer at roughly the same height, so the bars sit lower. That is a real change in feel and it stacks with your bar rise and your headset spacers.

On bar swaps, a front load faceplate comes off and the bars lift out forwards with the grips, levers and cables still attached. A top load usually means sliding the bars out sideways, which means stripping one end of the cockpit first. If you change bars often, that difference adds up.

Q067Will an old 1 inch stem fit a 1 1/8 inch fork?

No. The stem has to match the steerer diameter it clamps to or sits inside, and those two names describe different diameters. A stem made for the smaller steerer will not go over or into the larger one, and a stem made for the larger one clamped onto the smaller one has nothing to grip.

There is a second problem beyond diameter. Old school 1 inch stems are usually quill stems, which go inside a threaded steerer, and most 1 1/8 inch BMX forks are threadless, which need a stem that clamps around the outside. So the two are often incompatible in type as well as in size.

This guide does not publish the measured diameters for either name yet, because the inch names are nominal and the figures repeated online have not been checked. If you are matching parts, measure the steerer.

Q093When did BMX move from 1 inch threaded headsets to 1 1/8 integrated?

The sequence is clear and the dates are not. BMX went from threaded headsets with quill stems, to threadless headsets with clamp stems on pressed cups, to integrated headsets machined into the head tube, and steerer diameters got larger along the way. Any bike you pick up can be placed in that sequence by looking at the head tube and the steerer.

This guide does not publish a year for the changeover, because no source consulted gave one that could be checked. Era boundaries in BMX are generally described by which parts a bike has rather than by dates, and a lot of the year ranges repeated online are somebody's estimate that got copied. If you are dating a frame, use the parts and the construction rather than a headset year.
