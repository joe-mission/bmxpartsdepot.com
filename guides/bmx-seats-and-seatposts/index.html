Seats, Posts and Clamps

A seat has to match its post, the post has to match the frame, and the clamp has to match the top of the seat tube. Three separate measurements, three separate ways to get it wrong. This guide explains all three, with the post and clamp diameters sourced and stated rather than left to a guess at the counter.

Old SchoolMid SchoolModern

Quick Answer

Three things have to agree before a seat is on the bike: the seat has to match the post's interface, the post has to match the frame's seat tube bore, and the clamp has to match the outside of the seat tube. A pivotal seat only fits a pivotal post, a railed seat only fits a railed post, and neither cares what diameter the post is.

Not sure which kind of seat you are looking at?Read Pivotal, railed and tripod compared

Pivotal, railed and tripod

The first question about any BMX seat is how it attaches, because that decides which posts it will go on. There are three answers in common circulation and they do not interchange.

A railed seat has two wire rails moulded into its base. The post has a clamp head with a cradle and a top plate, the rails sit in the cradle, and one or two bolts squeeze them. This is how bicycle seats have worked more or less forever, and it is what you find on older BMX bikes and on most bikes that are not BMX. It gives you fore and aft adjustment, because you slide the seat along its own rails before tightening.

A pivotal seat has no rails. The base has a splined or serrated socket in it, the post ends in a matching splined stub, and a single long bolt drops down through the seat into the top of the post and pulls the two faces together. The serrations lock the angle. There is no fore and aft adjustment at all.

A tripod seat is the older three-bolt arrangement, with the seat base bolting to a plate on the post at three points. You mostly meet it on old school bikes and on the seat and post combos of that period.

None of these three convert to each other by buying an adapter you can trust. The interface is part of the post, so changing seat type means changing the post. This is the single most common seat buying mistake: a pivotal seat bought for a bike with a railed post, or the other way round, because the listing only said the colour and the brand.

Rail spacing is shared with the rest of cycling but rail diameter is not. BMX railed seats use 8mm rails against 7mm on road and mountain, while the cradle spacing is 43mm across all of them. So a road seat will sit in a BMX cradle designed for 8mm rails and be loose, and a BMX seat will not go into a 7mm cradle at all.

Pivotal parts do interchange between brands

This is a question people guess at, and it has a real answer. Pivotal is one licensed patent, originally MacNeil's, and every brand producing licensed Pivotal seats and posts builds to the same geometry: a 45-tooth directional spline stepping 8 degrees per tooth, with an M10 x 1.5 hollow bolt. Forty-five teeth at 8 degrees is 360, which is the arithmetic working out as it should.

That means a Pivotal seat from one brand fits a Pivotal post from another. The caveat that matters on used parts: this holds for licensed Pivotal products. Unbranded copies exist, and nothing guarantees a copy was cut to the licensed geometry. If a cheap unmarked seat will not seat properly on a known-good post, the seat is the suspect.

Pivotal seats

Quick Answer

A pivotal seat mounts on a splined face at the top of the post, held by one long bolt that passes down through the seat. The splines set the angle in fixed steps, which is why a pivotal seat has no fore and aft adjustment.

Pivotal exists because riders wanted the seat to stop moving. A railed clamp holds the seat by friction on two thin wires. Land on it hard enough, often enough, and it slips or the rails bend. The pivotal design replaces friction with mechanical interlock: two toothed faces that cannot rotate against each other once the bolt is tight, and no rails to bend.

The trade is adjustment. You get the angle you get, in whatever increments the spline pitch allows, and the seat sits where the post puts it. For most freestyle riding that is fine, because the seat is a thing you hold with your legs rather than sit on for an hour.

Setting one up is simple and there are two things to get right. Seat the splines properly before tightening, because it is entirely possible to pull the bolt down with the two faces half a tooth out and end up with a seat that looks fitted and is not. And do not treat the bolt as something to lean on. It is pulling two faces together, not clamping a load. Over-tightening it splits seat bases, and a cracked pivotal base usually starts around the bolt hole.

On a used pivotal seat, look straight at that bolt hole area and at the splines in the socket. Rounded or chewed splines mean the seat has been ridden loose. That seat will not hold an angle no matter what you do to the bolt.

Seat post diameter

Quick Answer

Seat post diameter is the outside diameter of the post, which has to match the inside of the frame's seat tube. More than one size is in circulation, so this is a measurement rather than an assumption.

This is the one number on the page that stops a build dead. A post that is slightly too big will not go in. A post that is slightly too small will go in, feel fine on the bench, and then sink or twist as soon as somebody sits on it, and no amount of clamp tightening fixes that. Crushing a seat tube trying to hold an undersized post is a way to ruin a frame.

Measure the post, not the tube. The post is easy: calipers across the outside, taken at a couple of points along its length and a couple of rotations round, because a post that has been over-clamped is often not perfectly round any more. If you only have the frame, measure the inside of the seat tube at the very top, and accept that a bore measurement taken by hand is less reliable than measuring an actual post.

Two things that catch people out. First, shims exist and are common, so a bike that currently has a post in it is not proof of the frame's native size. Check whether there is a sleeve in there before you order. Second, a used post often carries a size marking stamped or printed near the bottom, which is worth reading but worth verifying with calipers, because that marking may belong to a post that has since been shimmed, sleeved or swapped.

The clamp is not the same number as the post, and that is what people order wrong. A seat clamp goes around the outside of the seat tube, so its inner diameter is the post diameter plus two seat tube wall thicknesses. A 25.4mm post takes a 28.6mm clamp, and ordering a 25.4mm clamp for a 25.4mm post gets you something that will not go on the frame.

25.4mm is the modern freestyle default, near enough universally on current aftermarket frames and completes. If you are buying a post for a modern 20 inch bike without checking, that is the odds-on answer, but check anyway, because the older sizes are exactly what turns up on the used frames this site exists to sell.

Shims are made for the common step-ups, from Problem Solvers, Cane Creek and Gusset, so a smaller post in a larger seat tube is a solved problem rather than a bodge. Same rule as the bar shims: use a machined split sleeve, not a wrap of something.

Seat clamps, integrated and external

Quick Answer

An external seat clamp is a separate collar that sits over the top of the seat tube and squeezes it onto the post. An integrated seat clamp is built into the frame, usually as a machined boss with a pinch bolt, so no collar is needed.

The clamp does not hold the post directly. It squeezes the seat tube, which has a slot cut down it, and the tube grips the post. That is why clamp size is a different number from post size: the clamp has to fit around the outside of the seat tube, which is the post diameter plus two wall thicknesses.

An external clamp is a collar, sized to the seat tube, tightened with a bolt or a quick release lever. It is a wear part and a cheap one, and a bent or splayed clamp is one of the easier faults to fix on a used bike.

An integrated clamp is machined into the frame at the top of the seat tube. It looks cleaner, it cannot get lost or knocked off, and there is one less thing to bolt on. It also means that if the frame's clamp area is damaged or the bolt seizes and rounds out, you are dealing with the frame rather than swapping a five minute part.

A frame with an integrated clamp still takes a normal seat post. The clamp style tells you nothing about the post diameter, and the two questions have to be answered separately.

There is a third arrangement to recognise: a frame built with an integrated seat mast, where the post is effectively part of the frame or the seat bolts almost directly to it. That is a frame design decision rather than a clamp choice, and it constrains what seat you can run.

The pinch bolt

Either style of clamp works by pinching a slotted tube shut, and the bolt doing the pinching is the one part of the assembly that is under real load every time somebody sits down.

Two things are worth knowing about it. First, a pinch bolt is not a place to apply everything you have. If a post slips at a sensible torque, the answer is almost always the wrong post diameter, a missing shim, or a clamp that is already splayed, and none of those are cured by pulling harder. Riders who tighten their way out of the problem end up with a stripped thread or a snapped bolt head, and on an integrated clamp that becomes a frame repair.

Second, the bolt and the slot have to line up. If the slot in the seat tube and the gap in an external collar are on top of each other, the clamp closes the slot as it is designed to. If the collar is rotated so it is pinching across solid tube, it will feel tight and hold badly. That is a thirty second check that fixes a surprising number of slipping seats.

Post length, slammed setups and offset

Quick Answer

Freestyle riders mostly run the seat as low as it goes, which is called slammed, so post length is about having enough post left inside the frame rather than about height. A post that is too short to reach the bottom of the clamp is unsafe regardless of how tight the clamp is.

There are two ways to run a seat on a BMX bike and they want different posts.

Slammed means the seat is as low as the frame allows, sitting on or near the top of the seat tube. The seat is there to be gripped between the knees and to stop the frame from being a bare tube, not to sit on. Riders running slammed use a short post, and some frames are designed around it.

Up, meaning at a height you could actually pedal at for a distance, is normal on race bikes, cruisers and any BMX being ridden as transport. That needs a longer post and needs the minimum insertion respected.

Minimum insertion is the thing not to guess at. Most posts carry a maximum extension line, and it is there because the post is a lever: the further it sticks out, the more it is trying to bend and to pry the seat tube open at the slot. If a post has no marking, get a decent length of it inside the frame anyway, past the bottom of the clamp slot by a clear margin. A post snapped at the clamp line is a common used-parts fault and it does not happen without warning signs.

One more practical point on slamming. If the post bottoms out on something inside the frame before the seat is as low as you want, do not cut a post down without thinking about it. A shortened post has less material in the tube and moves your minimum insertion the wrong way.

The markings are not consistent, and the gap is exactly where you would not want it. Long posts carry a laser-etched minimum insertion or maximum extension line and publish a figure of 65 to 75mm. Stubby posts under about 150mm carry no marking at all and publish no minimum, because the design assumption is that they go in until they are slammed against the collar and stay there.

That is defensible for a post used as intended. It is a problem on a used bike, where you cannot tell by looking whether a short unmarked post is at a safe depth or just short. A long post tells you where the line is. A stubby post does not, and the absence of a mark is not permission.

So: if a post has a line, keep it below the line. If it has no line, get as much of it into the frame as the setup allows, and treat a post that only just reaches the clamp as the wrong post rather than a low seat.

Setback and zero offset

Some posts put the seat clamp directly above the centre of the post. Others set it back behind the post centreline. A zero offset post is the one with no setback, and it puts the seat further forward for the same frame and the same post height.

On a bike being ridden seated this changes where you sit relative to the cranks, which is a real fit consideration on a race bike or a cruiser. On a slammed freestyle setup it mostly changes where the seat is in relation to your legs when you grip it, which riders do notice.

If you are replacing a post and want the bike to feel the same, offset is the spec to check after diameter. Two posts of the same diameter and length can still put the seat in visibly different places.

Seat height and standover

Standover height is the height of the top tube above the ground at its lowest useful point, and it is a frame measurement rather than a seat one. It is worth mentioning here because riders confuse the two when sizing a bike.

Seat height is adjustable within the limits of the post. Standover is not, because it is decided by the frame. If a bike feels too tall over the top tube, a shorter post does not fix it.

Stuck and seized seat posts

Quick Answer

Posts seize because two dissimilar metals sit in contact with water in a slot cut down the seat tube. It is entirely preventable with a smear of grease or anti-seize on the post at build time, and unpleasant to reverse afterwards.

A seat post that will not move is one of the most common faults on a used bike, and it is worth understanding the mechanism because that is also the fix.

An aluminium post in a steel frame is a galvanic pair. Add moisture, which the seat tube slot lets in cheerfully, and corrosion products form between the two surfaces. Those products take up more room than the metal they came from, so they wedge the post in place. Time and a bike left outside do the rest. A steel post in a steel frame simply rusts itself in, which is slower and no better.

Prevention is one step at assembly. Clean the post, clean the bore, put a thin film of grease or anti-seize on the post, and slide it in. Do that and pull the post out once in a while to check on it, and this whole section stops applying to you.

Getting a seized post out is a job with a definite order to it, from least destructive upward. Undo the clamp completely and take it off, because people forget. Work penetrating fluid down the seat tube from the top and, on a frame with an open bottom bracket shell, up from underneath, then leave it for hours rather than minutes. Twist rather than pull, using the seat and post as a lever, because rotation breaks the bond where straight pull will not. Repeat, more than once.

Where it goes past that point, the options get destructive quickly, and the honest advice is to weigh the frame's value against the risk before starting. A post can be sacrificed, cut off and its remains cut out from inside. A frame that has had a seat tube split trying to save a post is finished. If a used frame comes in with a seized post, that gets said in the listing. Nobody wants that discovery at home.

Clamp bolts and plating

The bolt in a seat clamp lives outdoors, gets tightened often, and is usually the cheapest fastener on the bike. Most of them are zinc plated steel, a thin corrosion resistant coating that does its job until it is scratched through by a spanner or a tool that did not fit properly.

Two practical consequences. A rounded or rusted clamp bolt is a cheap fix on an external clamp and a much bigger problem on an integrated one, so on a frame with an integrated clamp it is worth replacing a marginal bolt before it becomes a job. And on old parts, plating condition is a genuine identification cue: bright, unmarked, obviously modern hardware in an otherwise period-correct bike is usually a replacement, which matters if you care whether a bike is original.

Seat and post material

Quick Answer

Posts are steel or aluminium, and seat bases are plastic with steel or aluminium rails or a moulded pivotal socket. The material tells you how the part fails, which is more useful than knowing which is lighter.

An aluminium post is lighter and will not rust. It is also the one that seizes galvanically in a steel frame, and it is less tolerant of being over-clamped, because a crushed alloy post stays crushed.

A steel post is heavier, cheaper, and tougher about being clamped. It rusts, which in a seat tube means it can bond itself in place just as effectively as galvanic corrosion does.

Seat bases are plastic, and the failure mode is a crack radiating from a bolt hole or from the point where the rails or splines are moulded in. That is where to look on a used seat, not at the cover.

Hi-ten and chromoly

Not all steel is the same steel. Hi-ten, short for high tensile steel, is the cheaper grade found on entry level bikes and on the parts that came with them. Chromoly is the alloy used where strength matters relative to weight.

For a seat post this shows up as wall thickness and weight. A hi-ten post does the job, it just needs more material to do it, and a cheap post that has been clamped hard is more likely to have taken a permanent dent at the clamp line.

Why the grade matters

Yield strength is the stress at which a material stops springing back and starts staying bent. It is the underlying reason the same part in two different steels behaves differently.

Below its yield point, a part flexes and returns. Past it, the deformation is permanent, which on a seat post means a permanent bend or a crushed section at the clamp, and on a rail means a seat that will never sit straight again. A dented or bent post is not something to straighten and sell on. It has already been past its yield point once and you have no way of knowing how close to failure it now is.

Combo seat and post units

A combo unit is a seat and post made as one piece, with no clamp between them. They are common on old school bikes, they turn up on complete bikes as a cost saving, and there is a modern version in the pivotal combo, where the seat and post are supplied and sold together and effectively stay together.

The advantage is that a joint that cannot come loose cannot come loose. Fewer parts, no seat slipping, and a lower stack height at the seat tube, which suits slammed setups.

The disadvantage is what happens when either half breaks or you want to change one. You are buying both. On old school bikes, the original combo unit is also frequently the part that decides whether a bike is period correct, which pushes prices for good ones up.

For fitment, a combo unit is still a seat post and the diameter question applies exactly as it does to any other post. Measure the shaft.

Buying a used seat, post or clamp

Seats and posts are cheap parts that get sold in poor condition more often than they should. What to look at, in order.

The post. Sight down it for a bend. Roll it on a flat surface. Look hard at the clamp line for a dent, a flat, or a step, and at the very bottom for the scoring and corrosion that says it lived in a wet frame. Check whether it is round with calipers rather than by eye.

The seat. Turn it over. The base is the part that matters and it is the part photos never show. Look for cracks at the bolt hole on a pivotal, at the rail mouldings on a railed seat, and along any moulded ribs. Check the rails are straight and parallel, because a bent rail will not sit in the cradle properly and cannot be trusted to hold.

The clamp. Check the bolt threads, check the clamp is still round rather than splayed open, and check that the slot has not been closed up so far that the clamp has been over-tightened onto a post that was too small.

Cover damage on a seat is cosmetic and honestly described as such. A cracked base is not, and it does not get sold.

Questions

Q061What is the difference between a pivotal, railed, and tripod BMX seat?

It is how the seat attaches to the post. A pivotal seat has a splined socket in its base and is held by one long bolt that passes down through the seat into the top of the post, with the splines setting the angle. A railed seat has two wire rails clamped in a cradle on the post head, which gives angle and fore and aft adjustment. A tripod seat bolts to a plate on the post at three points and is mostly an old school arrangement.

They do not interchange. The mounting interface belongs to the post as much as to the seat, so changing seat type means changing the post as well.

Q062Will a pivotal seat fit a railed seat post?

No. A pivotal seat has no rails for a railed post's cradle to clamp, and a railed post has no splined stub for a pivotal seat's socket to sit on. They are two different mounting systems, not two versions of one.

If you want to run a pivotal seat, you need a pivotal post. Buying both together is the normal way to change over, and it is worth checking the seat and post are actually sold as compatible rather than assuming every pivotal spline is the same as every other one.

Q059What diameter seat post does my BMX frame take?

Measure it rather than assume it. Take calipers across the outside of the post you already have, at a couple of points along its length, because a post that has been over-clamped is often no longer round. If you have no post, measure the inside of the seat tube at the very top and treat that reading as the less reliable of the two.

Watch for a shim. A frame with a post in it is not proof of the frame's native size, because sleeves and shims are common and are easy to miss until the post comes out. The diameters themselves are in seat post diameter: 25.4mm is the modern freestyle default, with 22.2mm on old school, youth and micro frames and 27.2mm on race and cruiser, all confirmed. Measure anyway rather than ordering off the odds, because a wrong number here is the one that gets a seat tube crushed.

Q060How do I measure a BMX seat post diameter?

Calipers across the outside of the post, taken at more than one point along its length and at more than one rotation. You are checking two things at once: what size it is, and whether it is still round.

Take the reading on a clean section away from any scoring or the clamp line, because a post that has been clamped hard often measures slightly under at the clamp and slightly over on the flats either side of a dent. If the readings disagree by any meaningful amount, that post is damaged rather than an unusual size.

Q063What size seat clamp does my BMX frame need?

The clamp fits around the outside of the seat tube, so its size is your post diameter plus two seat tube wall thicknesses. That means you cannot work it out from the post alone, and you should measure the outside of the seat tube at the top where the clamp sits.

Check first whether your frame has an integrated clamp, because then the answer is that you do not need one at all. The clamp size that pairs with each post size is in the table under seat post diameter, with its sourcing. Order from there, but measure the outside of your seat tube if the clamp does not close properly, because those pairings follow the tube sizes builders normally use rather than a standard tying the two together.

Q064How do I get a stuck seat post out of a BMX frame?

Work from least destructive upward and give it time. Take the clamp completely off the frame first. Work penetrating fluid down the seat tube from the top, and up through the bottom bracket shell as well if the frame is open there, then leave it for hours rather than minutes. Then twist rather than pull, using the seat and post together as a lever, because rotation breaks the corrosion bond where a straight pull will not. Repeat the soak and the twist more than once before escalating.

Past that point every option risks the frame, so weigh what the frame is worth first. The post is expendable and can be cut off and cut out. The frame is not, and a seat tube split trying to save a post is the end of it.

The real answer is prevention. A thin film of grease or anti-seize on the post at build time, and pulling the post out once in a while, stops it happening at all.

Q056What is an integrated seat mast and does my frame have one?

An integrated seat mast is a frame built so the seat mounts at or very near the top of the seat tube itself, rather than on a post that slides in and out. Look at the top of the seat tube: if there is no separate post visible and the seat sits effectively on the frame, that is what you have.

It is not the same thing as an integrated seat clamp, which is a clamp machined into the frame that still takes an ordinary sliding post. The clamp style and the mast are two different questions, and plenty of frames have the first without the second.

Railed seat

Quick Answer

A railed seat sits on two metal rails clamped by a matching post, and it does not fit a pivotal post. It is the main alternative to pivotal, and the two interfaces share nothing.

The rails run fore and aft under the saddle, and the post clamps them between two plates. That gives something pivotal does not: the saddle slides forward and back, so you can move it independently of the post. It costs weight and it raises the saddle, because the clamp sits under the shell rather than inside it.

For buying used, the interface is the whole question. A railed seat and a pivotal post are not made to work together and no adapter makes them equivalent. Check which one a seat has before buying it to go with a post you already own, and check the post the same way. Photographs of a saddle from above tell you nothing; you need the underside.

Worn rails are worth a look on anything second hand. The clamp bites in the same two places for years, and rails that are visibly flattened or grooved where the clamp sat have been overtightened at some point.

Rail diameter and cradle spacing are published above under pivotal, railed and tripod, sourced to Eclat and Shadow. The figure worth knowing before you buy is that BMX rails are thicker than road and mountain rails at the same cradle spacing, so a road post will not grip a BMX saddle properly even though the rails sit the same distance apart. Rails also come in round and oval sections, which changes what a given clamp will grip, so a seat and post you cannot try together are worth measuring.

Seat clamp diameter

Quick Answer

Seat clamp diameter is the outside diameter of your frame's seat tube, not the diameter of your seat post. It is a bigger number than the post, and it is the single most common ordering mistake in this part of the bike.

The post goes inside the seat tube. The clamp goes around the outside of it. So the clamp is always the larger number, because it has to clear the post plus the wall thickness of the tube on both sides. Ordering a clamp in the post's size gets you something that will not go on the frame.

Each post size has a clamp size that goes with it, and those pairings are published in the table under seat post diameter with their sourcing. Work from there rather than from the post number in your hand.

The pairings hold because BMX frame builders draw from a small set of tube sizes, not because there is a standard tying the two together. A frame built with unusually thick or thin seat tube wall will sit outside them. If a clamp bought to the table does not close properly, that is the reason, and the fix is to put calipers across the outside of the seat tube at the very top, where the clamp sits, and order to that measurement instead. If the frame has an integrated seat clamp machined into the tube, the question does not arise at all.

Tripod post spline

Quick Answer

Tripod is a third seat interface, alongside railed and pivotal, and it is not compatible with either. It uses a three-point mount rather than rails or a single bolt through a splined face.

You meet it rarely, which is exactly why it catches people out: a tripod seat looks close enough to pivotal in a photograph that it gets bought as one. The mount pattern under the shell is the tell, and it needs its own post.

The practical advice for a used purchase is short. If a seat or post is described as tripod, it works with tripod parts and nothing else, and replacements are harder to find than for either of the common systems. That is worth factoring into the price of a bike that comes with one.

The bolt pattern is published above under pivotal, railed and tripod: two front tabs plus one rear M8 x 1.25, taking a 6mm Allen key, sourced to Flybikes. That is one manufacturer rather than two, so treat it as one reading and check the part in front of you.
