Brakes, Cables and Detanglers

Most used BMX frames arrive brakeless, and most used brake sets arrive incomplete. This guide explains how each part of the system works and what has to be present for it to work at all. Post spacings and cable lengths are held back rather than guessed at, because those are the numbers people get wrong.

Old SchoolMid SchoolModern

Quick Answer

A BMX rear brake is a U-brake: two arms pivoting on two posts welded or bolted to the frame, pulled together by a straddle cable hanging from a yoke. If the bars need to spin, a detangler (gyro) sits above the head tube and splits the cable into two halves that rotate against each other.

Every part of that chain has to be present. A used frame with no mounts, or a used brake with no straddle cable and no yoke, is not a working brake, and those are the parts that go missing first.

U-brakes and how they work

Quick Answer

A U-brake has two arms that pivot on posts either side of the rim, crossing over each other so that pulling the straddle cable upward squeezes both pads inward. It is the standard BMX rim brake and it mounts to posts on the frame or fork.

The mechanism is worth understanding because it explains every setup problem people have with them.

Each arm is a lever. The bottom end pivots on a post. The pad sits partway up the arm against the rim. The top end carries one end of the straddle cable. The two arms lean inward across each other, so the left arm's top end is over on the right and the right arm's top end is over on the left. When the main cable lifts the yoke, both ends of the straddle cable are pulled up and inward, and the arms rotate about their posts to close the pads onto the rim.

Because the pull is upward on a triangle of cable, the shape of that triangle changes how hard the brake bites. A shallow, wide straddle gives more mechanical advantage and less pad travel. A tall, narrow straddle gives less advantage and more travel. That is why two identical brakes can feel completely different, and why a brake that feels wooden is often a straddle length problem rather than a pad problem.

Pad position is adjustable in more directions than most people use. The pad can move up and down the arm, in and out from the rim, and rotate to match the rim's braking surface. The pad face wants to sit fully on the braking surface, not hanging over the top edge of the rim and not touching the tyre. A pad that has been running half on the tyre is a used part that gets binned rather than sold.

Brake mounts and the 990 name

Quick Answer

Brake mounts are the two posts a U-brake bolts onto. On a BMX frame they sit on the seat stays or the chain stays. On a fork they sit on the back of the fork legs. The listing word for the standard BMX U-brake post arrangement is 990.

The name 990 is used in listings and in conversation to mean the standard BMX U-brake mount, and a brake described as a 990 brake is a U-brake meant for those posts. It began as a model designation and became a generic term the way a lot of BMX vocabulary does. This guide does not publish which model it originally referred to, or the post spacing figure, because neither has been checked against a source.

The identification job on a used frame is straightforward. Look at the seat stays just above the rear wheel, and at the chain stays just behind the bottom bracket. Posts on the seat stays mean the brake sits above the wheel. Posts on the chain stays mean it sits below and behind the bottom bracket, tucked out of the way, which was a common arrangement on frames built for riders who wanted a brake but not a brake in the way. Some frames have both. Many modern frames have neither.

Cantilever posts are not U-brake posts, even though they look similar at a glance. Both are a pair of posts either side of the wheel and both take a brake arm that pivots on them. The spacings differ, and the arms are shaped for different geometry, so a U-brake on cantilever posts will not sit where it should even if the arms slide on. The safe check is to measure the posts on your own frame and compare against what a brake is actually sold for.

Removable brake mounts

Quick Answer

Removable brake mounts bolt onto the frame rather than being welded to it, so a frame can be run cleanly brakeless and still take a brake later. They only help if you actually have the mounts, and they are the first thing that goes missing on a used frame.

The reason these exist is a genuine design tension. Riders who never run a rear brake do not want two lumps welded to the seat stays that catch clothing on a barspin and collect grind damage. Riders who do run a brake need somewhere solid to bolt it. Removable mounts settle it by putting threaded bosses on the frame and letting the mounts themselves bolt on and off.

The practical consequence is that a used frame advertised as "brake compatible" may or may not come with the parts that make it so. Look for the threaded bosses, not for the mounts. If the frame has bosses with bolts or plugs in them, the frame can take a brake and you need to source the mounts. If it has smooth stays with nothing on them at all, the frame is brakeless and no amount of hardware changes that.

Mounts are usually specific to the frame or at least to the manufacturer, because the boss spacing and the mount shape have to agree. A set of removable mounts off one frame is not automatically a set of mounts for another. That is worth saying plainly, because these turn up sold loose all the time.

The other thing a brakeless frame often lacks is a cable stop or cable guide on the top tube or seat stay. Without somewhere for the outer cable to terminate, a brake cannot be routed properly even with the mounts on.

Detanglers and how they actually work

Quick Answer

A detangler, usually called a gyro, lets the bars spin all the way round while the rear brake stays connected. It does this by splitting the brake cable into an upper and a lower half joined by two plates that rotate against each other on a bearing, with the cable tension carried through the bearing rather than through a single continuous cable.

Here is the problem it solves. On a bike with a rear brake and no detangler, one cable runs from the lever on the bars down to the brake on the frame. Spin the bars and that cable wraps around the head tube. A couple of rotations and the cable binds, the brake drags, and the bars stop turning. Anyone doing barspins, tailwhips, or an x-up past a certain point runs out of cable.

The detangler splits that cable in two. The upper cable comes down from the lever and terminates on the upper plate. The lower cable leaves the lower plate and goes on to the brake. The two plates sit one above the other on a bearing race centred on the steering axis, and they are coupled so that pulling the top one lifts the bottom one. Crucially they are not coupled rotationally: the top plate turns with the bars, the bottom plate stays with the frame, and the bearing lets them slip past each other indefinitely.

Because the split is at the steering axis, the upper cable never wraps around anything. The bars can rotate as many times as you like and the brake stays live throughout.

Two cables run down from the top plate on most designs, one to each side, so the pull is balanced and the plate is not dragged sideways when the lever is squeezed. That is why gyro cable sets come as an upper pair plus a lower single, and why an incomplete set is useless.

What goes wrong with them

A gyro adds friction to the brake system, and a badly set up one adds a lot. The usual faults on a used bike are worth knowing.

Bearing seized or gritty. The plates should rotate against each other with almost no resistance. If turning the bars pulls the brake on slightly, the bearing is binding and the brake will drag on every barspin.

Cable tension wrong. The upper cables have to be tensioned evenly. If one is tighter than the other the plate cocks over, the bearing loads unevenly, and the whole thing gets stiff.

Wrong cable lengths. A gyro needs cables cut for a gyro. A standard brake cable will not do the job of the upper pair, and cables that are too long loop badly and rub the bars.

Missing hardware. The small parts (the spacers, the mounting bolts, the cable ends) are what disappear when a gyro is pulled off a bike and thrown in a box.

Gyro tabs

Quick Answer

Gyro tabs are the small welded tabs on the head tube that a detangler bolts to, holding the lower plate still while the upper plate turns with the bars. Without tabs, a gyro needs a clamp-on adapter, and some frames have no provision at all.

The tabs exist because the lower plate has to be held to the frame. If it turned with the bars there would be no detangling. Two tabs on the front or back of the head tube give the lower plate two fixing points and stop it rotating.

On a used frame, tabs are easy to spot and easy to miss: they are small, they sit close to the head tube, and on a brakeless build they are often ground off and painted over. Look carefully at the head tube from the side. Grinding marks in the paint where two tabs used to be is a common find on a street frame.

If a frame has no tabs, the options are a clamp-on adapter that grips the head tube, or a gyro design that mounts differently. Neither is as clean as tabs, and a clamp-on adapter has to fit your head tube, which brings you back to measuring.

Straddle cable and yoke

Quick Answer

The straddle cable is the short cable that runs between the two U-brake arms. The yoke is the small carrier that hangs on it and takes the main cable from the lever. Both are frequently missing from a used brake set, and the brake does not work without them.

The yoke is a tiny part doing a lot. The main cable from the lever ends at the yoke. The straddle cable passes through the yoke or clamps into it, and its two ends anchor to the tops of the brake arms. When the lever is pulled, the yoke is lifted, and the straddle triangle closes, and the arms come in.

Where the yoke sits on the straddle changes the brake's feel, as described in the U-brake section. Raising it makes the triangle taller and narrower, which gives more pad travel and a softer bite. Lowering it does the opposite. This is a genuine adjustment, not a fiddle, and it is the first thing to try on a brake that feels wrong.

Some setups use a quick release at the straddle, where one end of the straddle cable unhooks so the arms can spring apart and let a fat tyre pass between the pads. That is the difference between changing a rear tyre in two minutes and letting the air out to get the wheel past the brake. On used brakes the quick release fitting is often the part that went missing.

When buying a used U-brake, the checklist is: two arms, two pads with fixing hardware, the straddle cable, the yoke, the springs, and the mounting bolts. A brake sold as "complete" that is missing the yoke is not complete, and a yoke is not a universal part you can grab off a shelf.

Brake levers

Quick Answer

A BMX brake lever is a short-pull lever made for U-brakes and cantilevers. Brake lever reach is how far the blade sits from the bar, sometimes adjustable with a small screw. The lever pull ratio matters more than the brand: a lever made for a linear pull brake will feel wrong on a U-brake.

The important distinction is pull length. A U-brake needs a certain amount of cable pulled for a certain amount of pad movement, and a lever is designed around that. A linear pull (V-brake) lever pulls considerably more cable for the same hand movement. Put a linear pull lever on a U-brake and the brake feels vague and the pads move too far. Put a U-brake lever on a linear pull brake and it locks up almost immediately with very little modulation.

Reach is the separate comfort question. Riders with smaller hands want the blade closer to the bar. Many levers have a reach adjustment screw that limits how far out the blade sits at rest. On a used lever, check that the screw is present and that it is not seized.

Two other used-lever checks. The cable adjuster barrel at the lever should turn freely and not be rounded off, because it is how you take up pad wear without touching the brake. And the lever clamp has to suit your bar diameter, which is a cockpit question rather than a brake question.

V-brakes and calipers on BMX

Quick Answer

A V-brake is a linear pull rim brake mounted on the same style of posts as a cantilever, with two tall arms pulled together directly by the cable and no straddle. It is real, it works, and it is uncommon on BMX rather than a BMX standard.

The difference from a U-brake is the force path. A U-brake pulls upward on a straddle triangle. A V-brake pulls sideways: the cable outer stops on a noodle on one arm and the inner cable crosses to the other arm, so squeezing the lever pulls the two arm tops directly toward each other. No yoke, no straddle, more cable pulled per unit of pad movement, and hence the need for a linear pull lever.

Where you actually meet V-brakes on BMX is on some cruisers, on race bikes, and on complete bikes that borrowed a mountain bike part. If you have one and it works, there is nothing wrong with it. Just remember it needs its own lever and it needs posts that suit it.

Caliper brakes (the single-pivot or dual-pivot type that bolts through a single hole in the frame or fork) turn up on old school BMX and on some cruisers. They need a drilled bolt hole rather than posts, so they are not an alternative to a U-brake on a frame with mounts. On a used old school bike, a missing caliper is a real problem, because the hole in the frame is the only thing you have to work with.

Cable routing and quick release

Quick Answer

Brake cable routing on BMX is deliberately simple: lever, down the bars, through the detangler if there is one, along a cable stop or guide, to the yoke. Route it so the cable never fouls the bars at full lock and never rubs the tyre.

The routing rules are practical rather than dimensional.

Nothing crosses the steering axis without a detangler. If a cable runs from the bars to the frame and there is no gyro, the bars can only turn so far. On a bike meant to be ridden with a rear brake and no barspins, that is acceptable, and a cable stop on the head tube keeps it tidy. On a bike meant to spin, it is not.

Cable outers want gentle curves. A tight bend in the outer casing adds friction that never goes away, and on a used bike you can often feel it: the lever pulls fine but the pads come back slowly. Kinked outer casing is a five minute fix and worth doing before blaming the brake.

The lower cable has to clear the tyre and the frame. On a rear brake, the run from the head tube back to the yoke passes over the top tube or along it, and it should be secured. On a fork brake, the cable comes down the front and needs to clear the tyre at full compression.

The quick release at the straddle earns its place here. Undo it and the arms swing out far enough for a fat tyre to pass between the pads. Without one, getting a wheel out means either deflating the tyre or slackening the whole cable at the yoke and setting the brake up again afterwards. Check for one before you buy a used brake set.

A keyed washer shows up in this area more than people expect. On some detangler and dropout arrangements a washer with a tab locates in a slot to stop a part rotating under load. They are small, they are easy to lose, and a setup missing one will work loose. If a used brake or gyro comes with odd little tabbed washers, keep them.

Brakeless frames and what gets left behind

Most used BMX frames come to a shop brakeless, because most street riding is done that way. That leaves a predictable set of traces, and knowing them tells you what the frame will and will not do.

No mounts, no bosses, clean stays. The frame was built brakeless. It will never take a rear brake without a clamp-on adapter, and clamp-on rear brake adapters are a compromise nobody enjoys.

Threaded bosses, no mounts. The frame takes removable mounts. Find the correct mounts and you have a brake bike. This is the good outcome.

Welded posts, no brake. Everything is there and the previous owner just took the brake off. Fit any U-brake that suits the posts.

Ground-off posts. Someone removed welded mounts with a grinder. This is common and it is a judgement call: the frame is now brakeless permanently, and how much material came off the stay in the process is worth a very close look, because a stay ground thin at the old post location is a structural question rather than a cosmetic one.

Ground-off gyro tabs. Same story at the head tube, with less consequence, because the tabs are not carrying load in the way a seat stay is.

Cable stops still in place with no cable. Harmless, and a useful signal that the frame was built to take a brake.

On the front end, a fork with no brake mounts is very common on modern forks and completely normal. See Q019 below for what to look for.

Questions

Q042What are 990 brake mounts and does my frame have them?

990 is the listing name for the standard BMX U-brake post arrangement. A frame with 990 mounts has two posts either side of the wheel, on the seat stays above it or on the chain stays behind the bottom bracket, and a U-brake sold as a 990 brake bolts straight onto them.

To check your frame, look along the seat stays just above the rear tyre and along the chain stays just behind the bottom bracket. Posts on either pair means you have mounts. Threaded bosses with bolts in them means the frame takes removable mounts and you need to find the mounts. Smooth stays with nothing on them means the frame is brakeless. This guide does not yet publish the post spacing figure, so measure your own posts and compare with what a brake is sold for.

Q043Will a U-brake fit a frame with cantilever mounts?

Not reliably. Cantilever posts and U-brake posts look alike, both being a pair of posts either side of the wheel, but the spacing and the arm geometry differ. A U-brake arm may slide onto a cantilever post and still put the pad in the wrong place relative to the rim, which is not something you can adjust your way out of.

The honest answer is to measure the post spacing on your frame and buy a brake made for that spacing. This page does not publish either figure yet because neither has been checked against a source, and a wrong number here costs you a brake that does not fit.

Q044What is the difference between a U-brake and a V-brake on a BMX?

A U-brake has two arms that cross over their posts and are pulled together by a straddle cable hanging from a yoke. A V-brake has two tall arms pulled straight toward each other by the cable, with a noodle guiding the outer casing into one arm and the inner cable crossing to the other. No straddle, no yoke.

The consequence is the lever. A V-brake pulls much more cable for the same lever movement, so it needs a linear pull lever, while a U-brake needs a short pull lever. Mixing them gives you either a brake that feels vague or one that locks with no modulation. The U-brake is the BMX standard. The V-brake is real but uncommon on BMX, mostly turning up on cruisers, race bikes and completes that borrowed a mountain bike part.

Q045What is a gyro detangler and do I need one?

A gyro splits the rear brake cable into an upper and a lower half, joined by two plates that rotate against each other on a bearing centred on the steering axis. The upper half turns with the bars, the lower half stays with the frame, and the brake stays connected no matter how many times the bars go round.

You need one if you want a rear brake and you want to spin the bars. If you ride brakeless, you do not. If you run a rear brake and never spin the bars past a rotation, you do not strictly need one either, and a plain cable with a stop on the head tube is simpler and has less friction. A gyro adds parts, adds friction, and adds setup, which is a fair trade only if you actually use it.

Q046How do I identify which version of gyro I have?

Start with what you can see: how many plates it has, whether the upper cables are one or two, how the lower plate mounts (tabs or a clamp), and whether the bearing is a sealed cartridge or loose balls in a race. Photograph the assembly from above and from the side before taking it apart, because the stack order is the thing people get wrong on reassembly.

This guide does not yet list the versions by name and generation, because that would mean publishing a brand history that has not been checked against a source. What is useful and safe to say is that gyros are not universally interchangeable part for part, so keep the hardware that came with yours together and buy replacements as a complete set where you can.

Q047What length gyro cables do I need?

Measure, do not guess. Gyro cable sets come as an upper pair and a lower single, and the lengths that suit your bike depend on your bar rise and width, your stem, and where the brake sits on the frame. Two bikes with the same frame can need different upper cables purely because one has taller bars.

The practical method is to route a piece of old cable or a length of string along the path the cable will actually take, with the bars turned to full lock in both directions to make sure nothing goes tight, then measure that. Cables slightly too long can be managed. Cables too short will pull the brake on when the bars turn, which is worse than no brake at all.

Q048Will any BMX brake lever work with a gyro setup?

Any short pull BMX lever will work with a gyro in the sense that the cable pull is the same. What changes with a gyro is the amount of friction in the system, because you now have two cable runs, two sets of cable ends, and a bearing between them.

That means lever quality and cable quality matter more on a gyro bike than on a plain setup. A lever with a smooth pivot, a working barrel adjuster, and good cable ends will make a gyro brake feel usable. What will not work is a linear pull lever, gyro or not, because it pulls the wrong amount of cable for a U-brake.

Q049How do I tell if used BMX brake pads are worn out?

Look at the grooves. A new brake pad has slots or a pattern moulded into the face, and when the face is worn flat and smooth to the point where the grooves have gone, the pad is finished. Also check the pad has not worn at an angle, which means it was set up badly and has been braking on part of its face.

Two things end a pad early regardless of thickness. Grit embedded in the rubber, which you can see as bright specks and feel by dragging a fingernail across, will chew a rim. And a pad that has been running over the top edge of the rim or against the tyre has a step worn into it and is scrap. Pads are cheap, rims are not.

Q019How do I tell if a BMX fork has brake mounts?

Look at the back of the fork legs, roughly level with where the rim's braking surface would be. Brake mounts on a fork are two posts, one on each leg, pointing backwards. If the posts are there, the fork takes a front brake.

Also check the fork crown or the back of the crown for a drilled hole, which would take a caliper brake instead of posts. Many modern BMX forks have neither, because most street riders run no front brake, and a fork with no mounts and no drilling will not take a front brake at all. Look for ground-off stubs and grinding marks too, since removing mounts with a grinder is common and leaves a mark.
