Old school, mid school and modern are **real and useful categories, recognised by the parts standards a bike uses**, not by a date stamped anywhere on it. **The year ranges attached to those words are contested**, so date a bike from its bottom bracket, headset, cranks and construction rather than from a number somebody put in a forum post.

## Why the year ranges are contested {#era-boundaries}

Search for what counts as old school BMX and you will get three tidy year ranges, repeated on site after site with real confidence and no citation. They may well be roughly right. We could not confirm any of them from a primary source, so they are not published here as fact.

Here is why the boundaries genuinely resist being pinned down.

**The eras are defined by part standards, and standards changed gradually.** Bottom brackets moved from American to Euro to Mid to Spanish, headsets from threaded to threadless to integrated, cranks from one piece to three piece. Not one of those changes happened on a date. Each one started with a few frames, spread through the aftermarket, and took years to reach complete bikes. A bike built during a transition genuinely has one foot in each era.

**Different parts of the sport moved at different speeds.** Race and freestyle did not adopt the same standards at the same time, and neither did the American and European markets. A bike that reads as one era by its bottom bracket can read as another by its headset.

**Complete bikes lag the aftermarket.** A frame built with a new standard reached riders well before the same standard showed up on a bike in a shop window. A complete bike is therefore always evidence of the older end of a transition, not the newer one.

**The words themselves were coined afterwards.** Nobody riding at the time called it old school. The categories were named retrospectively by collectors and by the used market, and retrospective categories tend to acquire firm-sounding dates that nobody actually agreed on.

**The sources that do give years do not show their working.** In the research done for this site, the only hard date anchor found in any technical source was a GSport article dated June 2004 which described the Spanish bottom bracket as still emerging at that point. Everything else was narrative: this standard came before that one, this one replaced that one, with no dates at all.

So this page treats era as a reading taken from the parts. That is both more honest and more useful, because when somebody hands you a frame it is the parts you have in front of you and not the year.

**On the last of those questions there is already an answer, and it is no.** Support documentation across twelve BMX frame manufacturers was audited and none defines the eras. No governing body does either. The terms are collector usage, circulated by communities like BMX Society and VintageBMX, which is why this page describes eras by their features and why the era chips across this site carry no dates. Three research passes have now looked. <span class="src src-conflict">Unpublished</span>

The year ranges themselves stay open below, and may stay open permanently.

### Three passes have now failed to source the year ranges {#three-passes}

This is the answer, and it is worth stating as one rather than leaving blank.

**Three independent research passes have looked for a primary source defining the era boundaries and none exists.** Contemporary catalogue archives, standards documents and racing sanctioning body rulebooks were all checked. The commonly quoted ranges, roughly pre-1988 for old school, 1988 to 2000 for mid school and 2001 onward for modern, are collector convention circulated by communities like BMX Society and VintageBMX. No manufacturer, standards organisation or governing body has ever published them. <span class="src src-conflict">Unpublished</span>

**A number that everyone quotes and nobody can source is worth knowing about.** It does not make the terms useless, it makes them vocabulary rather than fact, and this site treats them accordingly.

### Racing has dated documents, and still does not define the eras {#race-asymmetry}

There is a real asymmetry between the two halves of the sport, and it is not the one you would expect.

**Racing has always had sanctioning bodies publishing dated rulebooks**, the NBA from 1973, the NBL from 1974, the ABA from 1977 and the UCI from 1996. Those documents define equipment classes, 20 inch against 24 inch cruiser, and carry dated rule changes such as the NBL's double jump ruling in 1988. Freestyle never had an equivalent. <span class="src src-confirmed">Confirmed</span>

So the race side genuinely can be dated against documents where freestyle cannot. **But even the sanctioning bodies never defined old school or mid school.** Both halves of the sport borrow the same collector dates for era naming. The paperwork exists, it just never answered this question.

## Old school {#old-school-bmx}

::: quickanswer
Old school is the sport's **first generation of bikes and parts**, recognised by a large American bottom bracket shell, one-piece cranks, a threaded headset with a quill stem, and steel everywhere. **It is a parts description, not a date range.**
:::

The things that mark a bike as old school are mostly things that later disappeared.

**A big unthreaded bottom bracket shell with cups in it.** The American standard is the original BMX bottom bracket and it is the loudest single era signal on a frame. Research for this site confirmed the narrative from multiple sources: American came first, Euro followed as the first way to run three piece cranks in a threaded shell, then Mid, which kept the American bearing and did away with the cups, then Spanish. Not one of those sources gave a year for any of it.

**One-piece cranks.** A single bent steel piece forming both arms and the spindle, running in that big shell on loose ball bearings in threaded cups. Sheldon Brown describes one-piece cranks as mainly found on older American-made bicycles and on children's bikes made for the American market, which is a fair description of where you meet them now.

**A threaded fork and a quill stem.** Covered in its own section below, because it is the second loudest signal after the bottom bracket.

**Steel, and a lot of it.** Hi-ten frames were normal, chromoly was the upgrade worth advertising on a sticker, and parts were generally heavier and simpler than their modern equivalents.

**Period details.** Loose ball hubs with adjustable cones, plated steel hardware, tripod or combo seats, and often mag wheels or plastic wheels rather than laced spokes.

## Mid school {#mid-school-bmx}

::: quickanswer
Mid school is the **transitional era between old school and modern**, and it is the hardest to pin down because it is defined by change rather than by a settled set of standards. A mid school bike typically mixes an older bottom bracket with a newer front end.
:::

Mid school is where the sport's parts standards were being replaced one at a time, which is exactly why the boundaries are fuzzy and why the era is worth understanding through the transitions rather than as a fixed specification.

What is happening across this period, in rough order of how obvious each change is on a used bike:

**Bottom brackets moving away from American.** Euro appears as the first threaded three-piece option. Mid then arrives and keeps the American bearing while throwing the cups away, which is why a sealed American and a Mid share the same bearing to this day. Sources credit FBM with introducing Mid, and give no year for it.

**Cranks moving from one piece to three piece.** The frame's shell standard and the cranks in it move together, so these two changes are really one change seen from two ends.

**Front ends moving from threaded to threadless.** The quill stem goes, the stem starts clamping the outside of the steerer, and steerer diameter grows.

**Frames getting more purpose-built.** More gussets, more thought about brake mounts and cable routing, geometry starting to split properly between park, street, dirt and race.

The practical consequence for anybody buying parts is that **a mid school bike is the one most likely to need a compatibility check on every single part**. Modern bikes are largely one set of standards. Old school bikes are largely another. Mid school bikes are the mixture, and the only way to buy for one is to measure it.

## Modern {#modern-bmx}

A modern bike is the one where the standards have settled. A pressed-in bottom bracket, three piece cranks on a sealed spindle, an integrated headset, a threadless stem clamping the steerer, sealed cartridge bearings almost everywhere, and a cassette or freecoaster rear hub.

The dating problem inverts here. Old school parts are easy to place because they are obsolete. Modern parts are hard to place because current standards have been current for a long time and a bike from several years ago looks like a bike from this year. **Once a bike is in the modern era, the parts stop telling you the year.** Model-specific details, colourways and catalogue photographs are the only route from there, which is brand research rather than fitment.

That is worth saying plainly, because it is the honest limit of a page like this one.

## Dating a frame from its features {#dating-by-feature}

The reliable method is to take a reading from several parts of the bike at once and see where they agree.

Go through the frame in this order, because it runs from the strongest signal to the weakest.

1. **The bottom bracket shell.** Threaded or smooth, and how wide. This is the single most informative measurement on any BMX frame, both for era and for what will fit.
2. **The head tube.** Whether the headset threads onto the fork or clamps the outside of the steerer, and whether the bearings sit in pressed cups or drop into a machined seat in the frame.
3. **The cranks.** One piece or three piece.
4. **The dropouts and brake mounts.** Whether there are welded brake posts, bolt-on removable mounts, gyro tabs, or nothing at all.
5. **The construction details.** Gussets, the shape of the rear end, how the seat tube and top tube meet.

Then look for disagreement. **A frame whose features do not agree is telling you something real**, usually that it has been rebuilt with whatever was available, or that it was made during a transition, or that the frame and the parts on it are from different bikes entirely. On a used bike that last one is the most common answer by a wide margin.

### Serial numbers

The question everybody asks is whether the serial number gives a year. Usually it does not.

BMX serial numbers are typically stamped under the bottom bracket shell, sometimes on the dropout or the head tube. What they encode varies by manufacturer, by factory, and by period. Some makers used a date code, some used a purely sequential production number, some used the code of a contract factory that built for several brands at once. There is no universal scheme and no central register to look one up in.

So the honest answer is that a serial number is useful for two things: proving a specific bike is a specific bike, and matching against a manufacturer's own records if that manufacturer still exists and still holds them. It is not a year lookup.

**No BMX manufacturer publishes a serial dating scheme.** Twelve brands' support documentation was audited and none maintains a public lookup. Some factory stamps appear to carry dates, but no brand states that as a rule holding across production runs, so a serial can suggest a year and cannot establish one. <span class="src src-conflict">Unpublished</span>

Which is why the feature-based dating on this page is not a second-best method. It is the better one.

### Some brands really did stamp dates {#date-codes}

Not a lookup table, but real decodable schemes on specific brands.

**Schwinn stamped a two-letter date code** on the head tube or bottom bracket shell, running month and year: `AA` is January 1965, `BA` is February 1965, and onward. Raleigh's Nottingham factory stamped dates of its own. And 1980s and 1990s Taiwanese contract factories stamped a month and year prefix ahead of the sequential digits, so `G0392` reads as Giant, March 1992. <span class="src src-confirmed">Confirmed</span>

Factory prefixes are verifiable on frames, `G` for Giant, `AL` for Anlun, `F` for Fairly, but **no brand published a cross-reference key**, and brands sub-contracted across several factories within a single model year. So a prefix tells you which factory, and not reliably which bike. <span class="src src-conflict">Unpublished</span>

**These dates are worth more than the era labels**, and they come from a better class of source: dated catalogues and model year specifications, which are primary documents in a way a forum consensus is not.

Two things they let you do. A frame with a 1 inch threadless front end is unusual and narrow, sitting in a two-year window around 1994 to 1996 before 1 1/8 took over, so that alone dates a bike closely. And a complete bike with an American bottom bracket is more likely pre-2005 than after, while a Mid on a complete points the other way.

Note what these dates are and are not. They date **when a standard became normal on production bikes**, not when it was invented. Entry-level completes lagged the rest by years in both cases.

## Construction giveaways {#construction}

Beyond the part standards, how the frame is actually built gives the era away.

**The looptail.** A rear end where the seat stays and chain stays are formed from a continuous bent tube looping around behind the wheel rather than meeting at a separate bridge or dropout plate. It is a distinctive old school construction and one of the quickest visual reads on a frame from across a room.

**Wishbones and bridges.** How the rear triangle is tied together, and whether there is a brake bridge, changes with era and with what brakes the frame was designed around.

**Welds and finish.** Frame finishing standards changed over time, and the look of the welds around the head tube and bottom bracket is a genuine cue, although it is one that needs experience rather than a checklist.

**Brake mounts.** Welded posts, no posts at all, or bolt-on removable mounts. The move toward brakeless riding shows up in the frame itself, and a frame with no rear brake provision is placing itself firmly.

### Gussets {#frame-gusset}

A gusset is a reinforcing plate welded into a high stress joint, usually where the down tube meets the head tube or where the tubes meet the bottom bracket shell.

Gussets are a strength feature, and they are also an era and market cue. How much gusseting a frame carries, where it is, and whether it is a plate, a wrap or a formed piece all changed as frames got built for jumping and grinding rather than for racing. A heavily gusseted head tube is not an old school look.

### Unicrown forks {#unicrown-forks}

A unicrown fork has legs that bend inward and are welded straight to the steerer tube, with no separate crown piece. The alternative, older arrangement uses a distinct crown that the legs are brazed or welded into.

This is one of the cleanest visual generation markers on a bike, because you can see it from a photograph. It is not a precise dating tool on its own, but it separates fork generations and it is a good cross-check against what the frame is telling you.

## The front end: threaded to threadless {#threaded-to-threadless}

::: quickanswer
A **threaded headset** screws its top race onto a threaded fork steerer and is held by a locknut, with the stem sliding down inside the steerer. It is one of the strongest old school signals on a bike and it is **not compatible with a modern fork or stem**.
:::

If you look at one thing after the bottom bracket, look at the top of the head tube.

On a threaded setup, the fork steerer has threads cut into its outside, the headset's upper race screws onto them, and a locknut jams down to set the bearing preload. The stem does not clamp anything on the outside. Instead it drops down inside the steerer tube.

On a threadless setup, the steerer is plain, the bearings are preloaded by a top cap pulling on a star nut or compression plug, and the stem clamps around the outside of the steerer. That is the arrangement on essentially every modern BMX bike, alongside a headset whose bearings sit in a machined seat inside the head tube rather than in pressed cups.

The two systems share nothing useful. A threadless fork will not work with a threaded headset, a quill stem will not fit a plain steerer, and the diameters involved changed as well. **This is the change most likely to catch out somebody buying parts for an old frame**, because a fork bought on price will not fit a bike bought on nostalgia.

Note the steerer and the head tube are different measurements, and both changed. The steerer went up by 3.175mm and the head tube by nearly 7mm, so an old fork will not begin to fit a modern frame and the reverse is equally hopeless. There is no partial compatibility here to hope for.

The full headset side of this, including bearing seats and stack heights, is on the [headsets guide](/guides/bmx-front-end-headsets-and-forks/#bmx-headset-standards).

**Quill to threadless adapters exist, and no BMX manufacturer endorses them.** They are commercially made, a split wedge that goes into a 1 inch threaded steerer like a quill stem and presents a 28.6mm clamp body for a threadless stem. Velo Orange, Origin8 and BBB all make one. <span class="src src-confirmed">Confirmed</span>

**No BMX frame or fork manufacturer was found endorsing one for freestyle, street or dirt.** The concern is the wedge: it holds by friction inside the steerer, and a hard landing loads it in shear rather than the compression a quill was designed for. On a road bike that is a different question from a bike that gets dropped off things.

This is the same pattern as the pedal thread adapters on the [cranks guide](/guides/bmx-cranks-and-pedals/#pedal-thread-size). The part is real and sold for the job, and nobody who makes the parts it goes between has put their name to it. Here, unlike the pedal case, there is a stated mechanical reason to be careful.

### Necks, wedges and expander bolts {#bmx-neck-stem}

Old school riders called the stem a **neck**, and the word survives in listings for period parts. If you are hunting old school parts, search both words, because a lot of sellers still use it and a lot of buyers no longer do.

#### Wedge stems {#wedge-stem}

A quill stem is locked in place by a **wedge**, an angled block sitting at the bottom of the stem's shaft. Tightening the long bolt down the middle pulls the wedge up against the angled end of the quill, which spreads it out and jams it against the inside of the steerer.

That arrangement identifies an old school stem immediately, and it will not fit a threadless fork under any circumstances. There is nothing for it to expand against.

#### Expander bolts {#expander-bolt}

The long bolt that pulls the wedge up is the **expander bolt**, and it is the part of an old school front end most likely to be seized, rounded off, or missing.

It sits inside a steerer tube, which is a place water gets into and does not leave, and it is often the only fastener holding the front of the bike together. Two things follow. On a used bike, check the expander bolt head before you buy, because a rounded one means the front end has been fought with. And when a stem does come loose, expect a wedge that has corroded itself to the inside of the steerer rather than a bolt that simply undoes.

Getting a seized quill stem out is the same job in principle as a seized seat post: penetrating fluid, time, and rotation rather than brute pull. Drop the bolt a few turns and tap it down first to release the wedge, because that step is the one people skip.

## Mag wheels and period wheel details {#mag-wheels}

Mag wheels, meaning moulded wheels with a small number of solid spokes rather than a laced hub and rim, are one of the defining looks of old school BMX. Plastic and composite versions were common, and true cast alloy ones existed too.

For identification they are an excellent signal, because you can see them in any photograph. For riding they need thought. A mag wheel cannot be trued or rebuilt. What it does when it has taken a hard enough hit is crack, and a crack in a moulded wheel is not repairable. That means condition assessment on a used mag is a careful look over every spoke and around the hub area rather than a spin in a truing stand.

They also constrain everything around them. The hub is part of the wheel, so axle diameter, bearing type, brake surface and driver arrangement are all decided by the wheel rather than chosen separately.

### Cones and locknuts {#adjustable-cone}

Older hubs and older headsets run loose balls against **adjustable cones**, threaded cones that set bearing preload by how far they are screwed on.

This is a serviceable design rather than a disposable one, which is why old bikes can be brought back at all. It also means that on a used wheel there is a real adjustment to check. Too loose and there is play at the rim. Too tight and it feels rough or notchy as it turns. Both are adjustments rather than faults, up to the point where the bearing surfaces themselves are pitted, and a pitted cone or cup is a replacement.

#### Jam nuts {#jam-nut}

Once the cone is set, a second nut is tightened back against it to stop it moving. That is a **jam nut**, sometimes called a locknut in the same role.

If a hub keeps going loose no matter how carefully it is adjusted, the jam nut is the first suspect. If it keeps going tight, the cone was probably moved while the jam nut was being tightened, which is the classic way to lose the adjustment you just made. On a used wheel with an old loose ball hub, check that the jam nuts are actually tight before concluding the bearings are worn out.

## Buying and valuing vintage {#buying-vintage}

Vintage BMX pricing does not work like used parts pricing. It is driven by originality, completeness and rarity rather than by function.

**Originality.** A bike wearing its original parts is worth more than the same bike with better parts on it. That is the opposite of how the rest of the used market works, and it is worth internalising before you upgrade something you have just bought.

**Completeness.** The small parts are what get lost, and the small parts are what get hunted. Original hardware, the correct seat and post combo, the right pads, the right levers. A bike missing the pieces nobody kept is a bike somebody will spend years finishing.

**Condition against originality.** Original paint with honest wear is generally valued above a repaint, even a good one. A repaint removes evidence, and evidence is what a collector is buying.

**Rarity.** Some models were made in numbers and some were not, and that has more effect on price than condition does.

The practical result is that **the same frame can be worth wildly different amounts depending on facts that are not visible in a photograph**, and that anybody buying vintage as an investment is doing brand research rather than fitment research. This site is about fitment, so on that side it will point you at the parts and let you value the bike.

What we will say about our own listings is simple. Era gets described as what the part is, not as a year we cannot support. If a part is described as old school here, that means it uses old school standards. If we do not know what a part came off, the listing says so.

## Before you restore {#restoration}

Restoring an old bike is mostly a series of decisions you cannot undo, so it is worth making them in order.

**Decide what you are making before you touch it.** An original bike preserved, a rider rebuilt to be ridden, or a restoration to how it left the factory. Those three want opposite things, and the most expensive mistake in vintage BMX is stripping original paint off a bike that was worth more with it.

**Check the frame is worth the work first.** Look for cracks at the head tube and bottom bracket junctions, at the dropouts, and around any brake mounts. Check the rear triangle is straight and that the dropouts line up. Check for a seized seat post and a seized stem wedge, because both are jobs in their own right and both can end a frame.

**Work out what is missing before you buy anything.** The parts that are hardest to find are the small original ones. Knowing which of them you are short of decides whether the project is a weekend or a year.

**Take photographs before you take anything apart.** Cable routing, spacer order, which way round the hardware went. You will not remember, and on period parts there is often no reference to check against.

**Expect the fasteners to fight you.** Plated hardware that has sat outdoors for decades rounds off easily. Penetrating fluid and time beat force nearly every time, and a rounded bolt in an integrated fitting is a much worse day than a slow one.

**Keep everything you take off, including the broken parts.** Original hardware you cannot use is still a pattern for finding the right replacement, and someone else's project needs it.

The one thing we will say without qualification is the same thing we say about anything else: **if a frame or a part is cracked, bent, or sketchy, it does not get ridden and it does not get sold.** Age is not a reason to make an exception. A period-correct part that fails is still a part that failed.

## Questions {#questions}

::: faq Q090 | What years count as old school, mid school, and new school BMX?
This is the question everybody asks and the one this guide will not answer with numbers. Year ranges for the three eras are repeated all over the internet, and the research done for this site could not confirm any of them from a primary source. Nobody quoting them shows where they came from.

What the eras genuinely describe is parts standards. Old school means a large American bottom bracket shell, one-piece cranks, a threaded headset and a quill stem. Mid school means the transition, most often an older bottom bracket with a newer front end. Modern means pressed-in bottom brackets, three piece cranks, integrated headsets and threadless stems.

Read the bike rather than the calendar. It is more reliable, and it answers the question you actually needed answering, which is what parts will fit.
:::

::: faq Q089 | How do I work out what year a used BMX frame is from?
In most cases you cannot get a year, and you should be suspicious of anyone who gives you one confidently without naming the model. What you can get is an era and often a model.

Work through the frame from the strongest signal down: the bottom bracket shell first, then the head tube and headset type, then the cranks, then the brake mounts and dropouts, then construction details like gussets and the shape of the rear end. Where those readings agree, you have an era. Where they disagree, the frame has either been rebuilt or was made during a transition.

From there, a year normally comes from identifying the actual model against catalogue images and manufacturer material, not from the frame itself.
:::

::: faq Q058 | What is the difference between old school, mid school, and new school BMX frames?
The parts standards the frame is built around. An old school frame has a large unthreaded American bottom bracket shell for one-piece cranks and a head tube built for a threaded headset. A modern frame has a smaller pressed-in bottom bracket shell, a head tube machined for an integrated headset, and a threadless front end.

A mid school frame sits between the two and typically mixes them, which is why it is the era that needs every part checked rather than assumed. Construction changes as well: more gusseting, purpose-built geometry for park, street and dirt, and different provision for brakes as brakeless riding became normal.

The year boundaries between the three are contested and are not published on this site.
:::

::: faq Q091 | Can a serial number tell me the year a BMX frame was built?
Usually not. BMX serial numbers are normally stamped under the bottom bracket shell, sometimes on a dropout or the head tube, and what they encode is entirely up to whoever built the frame. Some are date codes, some are plain sequential production numbers, and some belong to a contract factory that was building for several brands at once.

There is no universal scheme and no central register. A serial number is good for proving a specific bike is that bike, and it is worth recording for that reason. For dating, the parts on the frame are more informative than the number under it.
:::

::: faq Q092 | What parts would a mid school BMX bike normally have on it?
The honest answer is a mixture, because the era is defined by the transition. Expect an older bottom bracket standard, often American or Euro, paired with a front end that is on its way to modern. Expect cranks moving from one piece to three piece. Expect frames with more gusseting and more purpose-built geometry than old school, and brake provision that varies a lot from bike to bike.

That mixture is the identifying feature. A bike where every part agrees with every other part is usually reading as old school or as modern. A bike where the bottom bracket and the head tube disagree with each other is usually mid school, or has been rebuilt.
:::

::: faq Q093 | When did BMX move from 1 inch threaded headsets to 1 1/8 integrated?
Gradually, and in two separate steps that people often merge into one: threaded to threadless, which is about how the stem attaches and how the bearings are preloaded, and pressed cups to integrated, which is about how the bearings sit in the head tube. Steerer diameter grew across the same period.

What matters for buying is that the systems do not mix. A threadless fork will not run in a threaded headset, a quill stem will not clamp a plain steerer, and the diameters changed as well, so measure before you buy anything for an older frame.
:::

::: faq Q094 | When did Mid bottom brackets take over from American?
The dated adoption figures are in the transitions table above, from catalogues and model year specifications. What those dates describe is when Mid became normal on production bikes, not when it was invented. The sequence is well supported: American came first, Euro arrived as the first way to run three piece cranks in a threaded shell, Mid followed and kept the American bearing while doing away with the cups, and Spanish came after that as a lighter alternative. Sources credit FBM with introducing Mid, without a date.

The single hard date anchor found in the research was a GSport article from June 2004 which described Spanish as still emerging at that point. That places Mid as already established by then and nothing more precise than that.

For a bike in front of you it does not matter much anyway. Measure the shell: a large smooth bore with cups is American, a smaller smooth bore with the bearings pressed straight into the frame is Mid or Spanish.
:::

::: faq Q100 | How do I tell a department store BMX bike from a real one?
Look at the bottom bracket and the cranks first. A one-piece crank in a large American shell on an otherwise modern-looking bike is the clearest single tell, because that combination stayed in use on cheap bikes long after it left real BMX. Then look at the steel: hi-ten frames are heavy, and a department store bike usually announces itself the moment you pick it up.

After that, check the details that cost money to do properly. Sealed bearings against loose balls in cheap cups, welded quality around the head tube and bottom bracket, whether the brakes are a recognisable BMX design, and whether any part on the bike carries a brand you can look up. A bike with no identifiable component brands anywhere on it is telling you something.

None of this makes an old bike a bad bike. Plenty of genuine old school bikes used one-piece cranks and hi-ten frames. The department store tell is that combination on a bike that is otherwise pretending to be current.
:::

::: faq Q095 | Is a used BMX part with surface rust still safe to run?
Surface rust on its own is usually cosmetic and it is normal on old parts. What matters is whether it is still surface rust. Rust that wipes back to sound metal and leaves light pitting is generally fine on a frame tube or a bar. Rust that has scaled, flaked, or eaten a visible dip into the material is not, and rust anywhere on a bearing surface, a thread, or inside a tube where you cannot see the extent of it needs treating as an unknown.

Pay particular attention to places rust concentrates because water sits there: inside the seat tube, inside the steerer around the stem wedge, inside the bottom bracket shell, and at the bottom of the fork legs. Those are also the places where the part is being loaded.

If cleaning it back leaves a part you would not put on your own bike, it does not get sold as anything other than what it is.
