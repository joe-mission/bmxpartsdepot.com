::: quickanswer
A set of BMX bars is described by four numbers: **rise, width, backsweep and upsweep**. **Rise is what most people shop on**, but the other three decide whether bars with the same rise actually feel the same once they are on the bike.
:::

## The four numbers on a set of bars {#bar-numbers}

Bars get sold on rise. That is the number in the listing title, and for a lot of riders it is the only one they look at. It is also the reason people buy bars that match on paper and then cannot get comfortable on them.

Think of the bars as one shape rather than four separate specs. **Rise** is how high the grip area sits above the clamp area. **Width** is how far apart the two ends are. **Backsweep** is how far the ends are angled back toward the rider. **Upsweep** is how far the ends tilt up out of the plane the bar sits in. Every one of those changes where your hands end up, and three of them change where your hands end up when you alter one of the others.

The interaction people miss is rotation. Because a bar has backsweep, rolling it forward or back in the stem does not just tilt the grips, it moves them. Roll the bars forward and the grips travel down and away from you, so you lose effective rise and gain reach. Roll them back and the grips come up and toward you, so you gain effective rise and lose reach. **A set of bars is not one position, it is a small range of positions, and the amount of backsweep decides how wide that range is.** Bars with a lot of backsweep change a lot as you rotate them. Bars with very little change hardly at all.

The second interaction is cutting. Trimming the ends of a swept bar takes width off, but because the ends are swept back and up, it also brings the grips very slightly down and forward. Nobody notices a small trim. Take a lot off and you have changed three numbers, not one.

## Handlebar rise {#handlebar-rise}

::: quickanswer
Rise is the **vertical height from the bar clamp area to the grip area**. It is the number that changes how tall the front of the bike feels and the one riders notice first when it is wrong.
:::

Taller bars put your hands higher and slightly further back, which makes the front end feel lighter and makes lifting the front wheel less work. Lower bars put more weight over the front, which most riders read as more stable and more planted, particularly at speed.

There is no correct rise. Height helps a bit, riding style helps more, and habit probably beats both. Somebody who has ridden the same rise for years will find a change of even a small amount obvious for a week and then stop noticing. That is worth knowing before you decide bars were the problem.

What matters more for buying is that **rise is not the only thing setting your hand height**. Stem stack height, headset spacers, fork steerer length and the bars' own backsweep all move your hands as well. If you fit bars with the rise you always run and they still feel low, look at what changed underneath them before you buy taller ones.

## Handlebar width {#handlebar-width}

::: quickanswer
Width is the **overall end to end measurement of the bars**, and it is the easiest of the four numbers to check on a used set. It is also the one most likely to have been changed, because cutting bars down is a normal thing riders do.

:::

Wider bars give more leverage over the front wheel and open the chest up. Narrower bars turn faster for the same hand movement and get through tighter gaps. Riders who spin bars often prefer them a little narrower simply because there is less bar to swing.

On used bars, width is the spec to treat with suspicion. A cut set is not a fault as long as the cut is clean and square and both sides match, but it does mean the listed model width is no longer the width you are buying. **Measure a used set rather than trusting the model name.** Uneven cuts are more common than people expect, and a bar that is a few millimetres longer on one side than the other will always feel slightly off centre no matter how carefully you line the stem up.

## Backsweep and upsweep {#handlebar-backsweep}

::: quickanswer
Backsweep is the **angle the bar ends are swept back toward the rider**, and it sets your wrist angle. It also decides how much the fit changes when you rotate the bars in the stem.
:::

Backsweep is the number nobody quotes and everybody feels. Two sets of bars with the same rise and width, one with a lot of sweep and one with very little, put your wrists at genuinely different angles. If bars feel wrong in a way you cannot describe, and the rise and width both check out, sweep is usually the answer.

It is also the number that makes bar rotation useful. Because the ends are swept back, rotating the bar in the stem swings the grips through an arc rather than just tipping them. That is free adjustment, and it is worth using before spending money. Set the bars, ride them, then move them a small amount at a time rather than making one big change.

The practical consequence for buying used: rotation means a set of bars close to what you want can usually be made to work, so do not rule out bars whose rise is slightly off. Rotation cannot fix width, and it cannot fix sweep.

### Upsweep {#handlebar-upsweep}

Upsweep is the angle the ends rise out of the plane the rest of the bar sits in. On its own it is the smallest of the four effects, which is why plenty of riders have never thought about it. Where it becomes noticeable is stacked with a lot of backsweep, because the two together decide how the grip sits under your palm rather than just where it sits in space.

If you have ever fitted bars that measured right and still felt like they were pushing your hands outward, upsweep is a good candidate.

## Two-piece, three-piece and four-piece bars {#bar-construction}

::: quickanswer
BMX bars are named for **how many pieces of tube are welded together to make them**. More pieces generally means a taller bar and more joints, not automatically a stronger or weaker one.
:::

A two-piece bar is a single bent tube with a crossbar welded in. Bending gets you a certain amount of rise before the tube starts to suffer, so two-piece bars tend to sit at the shorter end.

A four-piece bar splits the job up. The uprights are separate pieces welded to the crossbar and to the clamp section, so the maker is not limited by how far a tube can be bent. That is how taller bars are built, and it is why four-piece became the normal thing on freestyle bikes as bars got taller.

Three-piece and other counts exist and are exactly what the name says, an intermediate arrangement of the same idea.

The point people get wrong is treating piece count as a strength rating. **A weld is not automatically a weak point and a bend is not automatically a strong one.** A bend work-hardens and thins the tube on the outside of the curve. A weld adds a heat affected zone. Both are places a bar can fail, and which one fails first depends on the tubing, the butting and the quality of the welding, not on the number in the name. Buy on the maker's reputation and on the condition in front of you rather than on piece count.

### The crossbar

The crossbar ties the two uprights together and stops them flexing independently. On a two-piece bar it is doing that job for a single bent tube, on a four-piece it is a structural member the uprights are actually welded to.

Crossbar height, meaning how far up the bar it sits, varies between models and changes what the bars look like more than what they do. It matters for one practical reason: **anything you mount on the bars has to live around it.** A brake lever, a gyro cable, a phone mount or a number plate all need somewhere to go, and a high crossbar takes some of that room away.

## Bar clamp diameter {#oversize-bar-clamp}

::: quickanswer
The centre section of the bar has to match the stem's clamp. **Most BMX bars and stems share one common clamp diameter**, and an oversize option exists, so a bar and stem that will not close down properly are usually a size mismatch rather than a bad part.
:::

This is the one genuine fitment question on the whole page. Rise, width and sweep are preferences. Clamp diameter is pass or fail.

If the faceplate on a front load stem will not pull down evenly, or the bar spins in a tightened top load stem, stop tightening. Over-torquing a stem to hold an undersized bar crushes the clamp area, marks the bar, and can crack the stem. Shim kits exist for running a smaller bar in a larger clamp, and they are a legitimate fix when they are the right shim and not a strip of tin.

Going the other way, a larger bar simply will not go into a smaller clamp, and no amount of persuasion makes that a good idea.

### Knurling and clamp condition {#knurled-bar-clamp}

The knurled band in the middle of a bar is a roughened patch that helps the stem grip without needing to be crushed onto the tube. It is also the best condition tell on a used set of bars.

Knurling that is worn smooth in a band, or shows a clear polished ring, has had a stem slipping on it or has been clamped and unclamped a lot. Neither is fatal by itself. What you are actually looking for is **any dent, flat spot, ovalling or crack in the clamp area**, because that is where the stem loads the bar hardest and where an over-tightened stem does its damage. Run a fingernail across it. Sight down the bar from the end.

If a used bar is marked in the clamp area badly enough that the stem sits on a step rather than on a round tube, it does not get sold. That is the sort of part that fails without warning.

## Bar material and butting {#bar-material}

::: quickanswer
BMX bars are steel, and the meaningful difference between a cheap set and a good set is the **grade of steel and how the tube wall is butted**, not the shape. Butting means the wall is thicker where the loads are and thinner where they are not.

:::

A butted tube has different wall thicknesses along its length. The ends and the joints, where the stress concentrates, get more material. The middle of a span, where there is less going on, gets less. That is how a bar can be lighter than another bar without being weaker in the places that matter.

You will see butting described in listings as double, triple or quad butted, meaning the number of distinct wall thicknesses along the tube. The trap is that this is a marketing term as much as an engineering one. **A quad butted bar from a maker who does it properly is a genuinely good part. The same words on a bar with no named tubing behind them mean whatever the seller wants them to mean.** The words on their own are not a spec.

For a used buyer, the honest position is that you cannot see butting from outside the tube and you cannot weigh it out of a listing photo. What you can do is buy on the maker, check the clamp area and the welds, and treat unbranded bars with no model name as an unknown.

Aluminium BMX bars exist and are unusual. If a set of bars is noticeably light in the hand and does not attract a magnet, that is worth knowing before you fit pegs to the front wheel and start putting the bike through what a steel bar is expected to take.

## Grips, flanges and barends {#bmx-grips}

::: quickanswer
Grips have to match the **outside diameter of the bar end**, and BMX bars are consistent enough that most grips fit most bars. The real choices are **flanged or flangeless**, how long the grip is, and what you put in the open end of the bar.
:::

A flange is the raised collar at the inboard end of a grip. It stops your hand sliding inward and it gives a definite edge to locate against. Flangeless grips leave the bar clean, which riders who move their hands around or who mount things close to the grip usually prefer. This is preference, not fitment, and riders are firm about it in both directions.

Grip length matters more than people expect on a cut set of bars, because a long grip on a short bar end leaves nowhere for a brake lever. Check the lever position before deciding a grip is too long, not after.

### Barends

The open end of a handlebar is a small circular cutting edge, and it is the single most avoidable injury on a BMX bike. **Barends are not optional.** Alloy barends wedge into the tube and are the normal fitting. Plastic push-in ends do the same job more cheaply. Grips with a moulded closed end are also fine as long as the end is genuinely closed and not just a thin skin.

A used bike with no barends fitted has been ridden with no barends fitted, which is worth remembering when you are judging how the rest of it was looked after.

## Stems and where they put the bars {#front-load-stem}

::: quickanswer
A **front load stem clamps the bars with a removable faceplate**, so bars come off without pulling the grips or the levers. A top load clamps from above and generally sits the bars a little higher for the same stem.
:::

The stem is the other half of the cockpit and it moves your hands as surely as bar rise does. Two things about it matter for fit: how far forward it puts the bars, and how much height it adds under them.

Front load is the convenient one. Undo the faceplate bolts and the bars lift straight out, so swapping bars does not mean stripping grips, levers and gyro cables off. Top load usually stacks a little taller for a given model, which riders who want maximum hand height use deliberately.

If you are chasing a specific hand position, remember the stack adds up: fork steerer length, headset, spacers, stem, then bar rise. Changing the stem changes the same total that changing the bars does. That is why the honest advice on a cockpit that feels wrong is to work out which part of the stack changed rather than immediately buying taller bars.

When you refit bars, tighten the faceplate bolts evenly in a cross pattern and leave an even gap top and bottom. A faceplate pulled down hard on one side first is how clamp areas get marked and how faceplates crack.

## Questions {#questions}

::: faq Q068 | How do I measure BMX bar rise, width, backsweep, and upsweep?
Stand the bars on a flat surface resting on the clamp area, with the bar sat square. Rise is the vertical height from that surface up to the grip area. Width is the straight line measurement from one end to the other, taken across rather than around the bend.

The two sweeps are angles rather than lengths. Backsweep is read looking down on the bar from above, as the angle the ends make against the line of the clamp section. Upsweep is read looking at the bar from the front, as the angle the ends rise out of the bar's plane. Both are easier to compare between two sets of bars than to measure precisely on one.

The most common error is not sitting the bar square before taking the rise reading. A bar tipped even slightly gives a rise figure that is out by a useful amount.
:::

::: faq Q069 | What bar rise should I run on a BMX bike?
There is no correct answer, and anyone quoting one number for your height is guessing. Taller bars lift your hands and make the front end easier to bring up. Lower bars put more weight over the front and feel more planted.

The useful starting point is what you are already riding. Measure your current bars and change one step from there rather than jumping. Also check what else in the stack changed, because stem height, spacers and fork steerer length move your hands just as much as rise does.
:::

::: faq Q065 | Do all BMX bars use the same clamp size?
Almost, but not quite. One clamp diameter covers most BMX bars and most BMX stems, and an oversize option also exists, so a bar that will not clamp properly is usually a size mismatch rather than a damaged part.

If a faceplate will not pull down evenly, or the bar turns in a properly tightened stem, stop and check the sizes rather than tightening harder. Over-torquing a stem onto an undersized bar crushes the clamp area and can crack the stem. Shims are a legitimate way to run a smaller bar in a larger clamp when they are the correct part.
:::

::: faq Q070 | What is the difference between 2 piece, 3 piece, and 4 piece BMX bars?
It is the number of pieces of tube welded together to make the bar. A two-piece bar is one bent tube plus a crossbar. A four-piece bar has separate uprights welded to the crossbar and to the clamp section, which is how taller bars get built without bending a tube further than it wants to go.

Piece count is not a strength rating. A bend thins and work-hardens the tube on the outside of the curve, and a weld leaves a heat affected zone. Both are places a bar can fail. Tubing quality, butting and weld quality decide durability, not the number in the name.
:::

::: faq Q071 | Can I cut BMX bars down and how much is safe to remove?
Riders cut bars routinely and it is a normal modification, but there are limits and this guide is not going to invent a figure for them. What is certain is that the cut has to be square, deburred, and identical on both sides, and that you need enough straight bar end left for the grip and the lever.

Two things to think about before cutting. Because the ends are swept, cutting a lot off does not only change width, it brings the grips slightly down and forward as well. And a cut cannot be undone, so cut less than you think you want and ride it before going further.

The amount of bar end a maker considers usable is a specification we have not confirmed, so it is listed on this page as needing a source rather than answered with a number.
:::

::: faq Q072 | What is the difference between flanged and flangeless BMX grips?
A flange is the raised collar at the inboard end of the grip. Flanged grips give your hand a definite stop and stop it sliding inward. Flangeless grips leave the bar clean next to the stem area, which suits riders who move their hands around or who mount a lever tight to the grip.

Both fit the same bars. This is a preference, not a fitment question. The one practical difference is that a flange takes up bar length, so on a cut set of bars it can compete with the brake lever for room.
:::

::: faq Q073 | How do I get old BMX grips off without wrecking them?
Get something slippery between the grip and the bar. A thin screwdriver or a zip tie slid under the lip, then a squirt of soapy water or window cleaner, will usually let the grip twist off in one piece. Compressed air into the gap does the same job faster if you have a compressor.

Do not start with heat, and do not start by cutting, because both remove the option of reusing the grip. If the grip is glued on and refuses to move, cutting is the fallback rather than the first move. Let the bar dry before fitting new grips, or they will spin.
:::

::: faq Q066 | What is the difference between a front load and a top load BMX stem?
A front load stem clamps the bars with a removable faceplate, so the bars come out without stripping the grips and levers off. A top load stem clamps from above, and for a given model usually stacks a little taller, which riders chasing hand height use deliberately.

Both hold the same bars, assuming the clamp diameters match. The choice is about how the bars are held and how much height the stem adds under them, not about compatibility.
:::
