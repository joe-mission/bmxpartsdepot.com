::: quickanswer
**Measure the inside diameter of your frame's bottom bracket shell.** That single number tells you which standard you have: roughly **51.3 to 51.5mm is American**, **41.3mm is Mid**, **37mm is Spanish**, and a **threaded shell at 1.37 inch x 24 TPI is Euro**.

The shell standard sets the bearing **outer** diameter. Your cranks set the bearing **bore**. Those are two separate numbers, and matching both is the whole job.
:::

:::

## How to identify your bottom bracket {#identify}

Pull the cranks and look at the shell. You are trying to answer two questions: is the shell threaded or smooth, and how wide is the hole.

If the shell has threads cut into it, you have a Euro. If it is smooth, you have one of the three press-fit standards, and the diameter separates them. The gap between Spanish at 37mm and Mid at 41.3mm is over 4mm, and the gap between Mid and American is nearly 10mm, so you do not need laboratory precision here. A caliper across the bore, or even a tape measure held carefully, will put you in the right bracket.

One thing that catches people out: some sources quote the bearing or cup outer diameter rather than the shell bore, and on a sealed American bottom bracket those are different numbers. If your measurement lands between the standards, you are probably measuring a cup that is already pressed in rather than the frame itself.

## American bottom bracket {#american-bottom-bracket}

::: quickanswer
An **American bottom bracket** uses the largest shell of the four BMX standards, with sources placing the bore between **51.3mm and 51.5mm**. It runs either threaded cups with loose ball bearings for one-piece Ashtabula cranks, or pressed-in cups holding cartridge bearings for three-piece cranks.
:::

American is the original BMX standard and the one you meet on old school bikes and on department store bikes with one-piece cranks. It survived into the mid-school era before Mid and Spanish displaced it.

The shell diameter is the one figure on this page where good sources genuinely disagree. Park Tool and TBB-BIKE both state 51.5mm. Sheldon Brown states 51.3mm, given as 2.02 inches. SkatePro rounds to 51mm. Part of that spread is almost certainly the cup-versus-bore problem described above, so treat American as a range rather than a fixed number and do not buy parts on the basis of a 0.2mm distinction.

The detail worth knowing is that a **sealed American bottom bracket uses the same cartridge bearing as a Mid**, 41.3mm outer diameter and 11mm wide. The cup makes up the difference between the 41.3mm bearing and the much larger shell. That is why an American to Mid conversion is possible at all: you are not changing the bearing, only removing the adapter around it.

**This is where the published disagreement comes from.** Sources give American as 51.3mm, 51.5mm or 51mm, and both readings above are correct measurements of the same frame. The bare shell reads 51.35. A cup pressed into it reads 51.52, because a press-fit cup is deliberately made larger than the hole it goes into, here by 0.17mm.

So a source quoting 51.5 was almost certainly measuring across a cup, and a source quoting 51.3 was measuring the frame. Neither is wrong, and neither is measuring the same thing. **Pull the cups before you measure**, and if your reading lands near 51.5 on a shell you believe is empty, look again for a cup still seated in it.
:::

## Mid bottom bracket {#mid-bottom-bracket}

::: quickanswer
A **Mid bottom bracket** has a **41.3mm (1 5/8 inch)** smooth shell, and the sealed cartridge bearings press directly into the frame with no cups. It is the most common standard on modern BMX bikes.
:::

Mid is the standard that took over. It kept the American bearing and threw away the cups, which saved weight and removed a part that could work loose. Sources credit FBM with introducing it, though none of them give a year, so this page does not either.

Five independent sources give the shell diameter as the same number to rounding: 41.2, 41.25, 41.275, 41.3 and 41mm. The exact value is 1 5/8 inch, which converts to 41.275mm. Published here as 41.3mm.

## Spanish bottom bracket {#spanish-bottom-bracket}

::: quickanswer
A **Spanish bottom bracket** has a **37mm** smooth shell and, like Mid, takes cartridge bearings pressed directly into the frame. It is the lighter alternative to Mid and appeared in the mid 2000s.
:::

Spanish works exactly the way Mid does. The only difference that matters is the diameter, 37mm against Mid's 41.3mm, which means a smaller and lighter bearing with less material around the bore. Six sources agree on 37mm, so this is one of the most solid figures on the page.

A note on part numbers. One retailer lists Spanish bearings as "6904", but a stock 6904 bearing is 20 x 37 x 9mm, a 20mm bore, which cannot be right for a 19mm or 22mm spindle. Another lists "22379", which reads as 22 x 37 x 9 and is dimensionally coherent. **Buy Spanish bearings by their dimensions, not by a generic bearing number.** They are a special size and an ordinary bearing shop will not have them on the shelf.

You will also see 24mm listed against Spanish on some size charts. Only one source did so, and at a 37mm outer diameter the wall left around a 24mm bore is very thin. This guide does not present 24mm Spanish as a normal option.

## Euro bottom bracket {#euro-bottom-bracket}

::: quickanswer
A **Euro bottom bracket** is the only threaded BMX standard, cut at **1.37 inch x 24 TPI**, which is the same thread as English or BSA. Threaded cups screw into the frame and carry the bearings inside them.
:::

Euro arrived as the first way to run three-piece cranks without the American cup arrangement. On freestyle frames it is now uncommon. On race frames it is alive and well, usually as an external bearing system running a 24mm spindle.

The era column is deliberately vague about invention dates, because no source gives a year for when any of these standards was created. Sources describe the sequence clearly, American first, then Euro, then Mid, then Spanish, and they name the frames, and none of them dates it.

What can now be dated is **adoption**, which is a different claim and a more useful one. Dated catalogues put Mid on aftermarket frames from 2002 to 2004 and make it the default on complete bikes between 2005 and 2007, with American holding on at entry level until around 2007. The GSport article from June 2004 describing Spanish as still emerging sits neatly inside that. The full transition table is on the [era guide](/guides/vintage-bmx-identification/#transitions).

So: if a complete bike has an American shell it is more likely to predate 2005 than follow it. That is a probability from catalogue evidence, not a rule.

## Crank spindle diameter {#crank-spindle-diameter}

::: quickanswer
BMX freestyle cranks use **19mm or 22mm spindles**, with **24mm** mostly found on race setups. The spindle diameter is the **bore** of your bottom bracket bearing, a completely separate number from the shell diameter.
:::

19mm is the default. It is what most complete bikes ship with and what most aftermarket cranks use, and sources describe it as plenty strong for normal riding. 22mm is the durability upgrade, and sources describe it as the size the industry settled on after experimenting more widely.

24mm is where the received wisdom goes wrong. It is usually presented as simply the strongest option. The one source that addressed the tradeoff directly says the opposite in part: going to 24mm grows the bearing bore while the shell fixes the outer diameter, so you gain spindle strength and lose bearing material. In current BMX, 24mm is mostly a race and external bearing thing rather than a freestyle Mid thing.

Two things worth carrying away. First, **"19, 22, 24" is the complete set for freestyle, not for BMX as a whole.** Race also uses 30mm and 35mm. Second, **a 24mm spindle is really 15/16 inch, or 23.81mm.** If you put calipers on a used 24mm spindle and read 23.8, it is not worn, it is correctly sized.

## How bore and outer diameter work together {#sealed-bearing-sizes}

::: quickanswer
A bottom bracket bearing has two diameters that matter. The **outer diameter is set by your frame's shell standard**. The **bore is set by your cranks' spindle**. You match both, and you change bearings rather than frames.
:::

This is the single idea that makes everything else on this page usable. Say it out loud once and the compatibility questions answer themselves.

So a Mid frame with 22mm cranks needs a bearing that is 41.3mm on the outside and 22mm through the middle. A Spanish frame with the same cranks needs 37mm outside and 22mm through the middle. Same cranks, different bearing, no change to either the frame or the cranks.

## Swapping standards and spindles {#compatibility}

You cannot change your frame's shell standard, but you can usually run the cranks you want in the frame you have.

**Different spindle in the same frame.** Change the bearings to ones with the right bore. The outer diameter stays the same because the frame has not changed. Bottom bracket kits ship with a spacer set to sort the chainline out afterwards.

**American frame, modern cranks.** Adapter cups reduce the shell down to the Mid bearing, which is the same bearing an American already uses inside its own cups. This is the most common conversion on an old frame.

**Sprocket bore.** Sprockets are frequently bored for 24mm (15/16 inch), with a small adapter dropping that down to 22mm or 19mm. Some are simply bored 19mm. Check before assuming a sprocket will slide on.

**Shell width.** Mid and Spanish kits are sold to cover both 68mm and 73mm shells, with the spacer set making up the difference. Width is worth measuring, but it rarely stops a build.

One deliberate omission: this guide does not publish a grid asserting that a given spindle cannot physically fit a given shell. No source found stated a hard upper limit per standard, and the honest answer is to buy what manufacturers actually list for your combination rather than trusting a matrix somebody drew from first principles.

## Cross-brand compatibility {#cross-brand}

Bottom bracket bearings are dimensional parts, not proprietary ones. ## Bottom bracket shell width {#bottom-bracket-shell-width}

::: quickanswer
**Shell width is the measurement between the two outer faces of the bottom bracket shell**, across the frame rather than through it. On BMX it is **68mm** almost without exception. It is a different number from shell diameter, and diameter is the one that picks your standard.
:::

Width matters less than diameter, because Mid and Spanish kits are sold with spacer sets that cover the range. It is still worth a reading on any used frame, for one reason: **you are checking that nobody has ground the shell faces down.** A previous owner fighting a stuck bearing sometimes takes material off the face, and a shell that is narrow because it has been attacked with a file will not hold a bearing square.

The figures already in the tables above hold. 68mm is the BMX answer, 73mm turns up on MTB and dirt jump frames, and Mid and Spanish kits are commonly sold to suit both.

## Press-fit bottom brackets and how they go wrong {#press-fit-bottom-bracket}

::: quickanswer
A **press-fit bottom bracket** has its bearings pressed directly into the frame shell rather than threaded into it. Mid, Spanish and sealed American are all press-fit. The bearing is held by friction against an interference fit, which is why how it goes in matters as much as what goes in.
:::

Use a threaded bearing press with stepped drifts that bear on the **outer** race. That is the whole technique. The press pulls the bearing in square and slowly, and the drift makes sure the load goes through the part of the bearing that can take it.

What goes wrong is almost always a hammer. Driving a bearing in with a socket and a mallet takes it in at an angle, and three things follow. The shell can be ovalised, which is permanent and takes the frame out of service for that standard. The seal can be pushed in or nicked, which turns a sealed bearing into an unsealed one. And on a setup with an internal spacer tube between the bearings, over-driving crushes the spacer, after which the bearings are preloaded against each other and will not spin freely no matter what you do to the cranks.

**On a used frame, look at the shell before you look at anything else.** A bore that is bright and scored, or that will not take a new bearing with normal press force, has been hammered.

## Loose ball bearings, and when to bin them {#loose-ball-bearings}

::: quickanswer
**Loose ball bearings** run directly against cup and cone races rather than living inside a sealed cartridge. You meet them on one-piece cranks, vintage headsets and older hubs. Whether they are worth servicing depends entirely on the race, not on the balls.
:::

The balls are consumable and cost almost nothing. The **race** is the part pressed into the component body, and it is the part that decides.

So the rule here is simple. Run a fingernail around the cup race and look at it in good light. **If the race is smooth, the part is worth servicing**, and new balls plus fresh grease will make it run properly again. **If the race is pitted, scored or has a visible track worn into it, bin the part.** New balls in a pitted race feels fine for a week and then goes rough again, because every ball is rolling over the same damage. That is a temporary fix sold as a repair, and it is not something that goes out with a part from here.

## Unsealed bearings on used parts {#unsealed-bearings}

::: quickanswer
**Unsealed** means the bearing has no cartridge and no seal, so grit and water reach the balls directly. It is the older arrangement and it is fully serviceable, which is the upside. The downside is that neglect does permanent damage rather than temporary damage.
:::

The check is the same one as for loose balls above, because it is the same failure. A hub or bottom bracket with unsealed bearings gets listed here only when the cup races are clean. Rust pitting or scoring in the race means the part is scrapped rather than rebuilt.

Worth being blunt about why. A sealed cartridge that has gone rough is a ten minute fix, because you press the old one out and a new one in and the frame or hub is untouched. **An unsealed part with a damaged race is not repairable in the same way**, because the race is part of the component. That is the real difference between the two systems on the used market, and it is worth more than any argument about which spins better when new.

## Questions {#questions}

::: faq Q001 | What bottom bracket does my BMX frame take?
Measure the inside diameter of the bottom bracket shell with the cranks out. Roughly 51.3 to 51.5mm is American, 41.3mm is Mid, 37mm is Spanish. If the shell has threads cut into it rather than a smooth bore, it is Euro at 1.37 inch x 24 TPI.

If your reading falls between two standards, check whether a cup is still pressed into the shell. On a sealed American bottom bracket the cup and the shell are different diameters, and measuring the cup will give you a misleading number.
:::

::: faq Q002 | What is the difference between American, Mid, and Spanish bottom brackets?
Diameter, and whether there are cups. American is the largest at roughly 51.3 to 51.5mm and holds its bearings inside cups that fit into the frame. Mid is 41.3mm and the bearings press straight into the frame with no cups. Spanish is 37mm and works the same way as Mid, just smaller and lighter.

The useful detail is that Mid and sealed American use the same bearing, 41.3mm outer diameter and 11mm wide. American just wraps a cup around it to fill the larger shell.
:::

::: faq Q003 | How do I measure a BMX bottom bracket shell?
Take the cranks and the bottom bracket out, then measure across the inside of the shell with calipers. Take the reading at a few points around the bore, because a shell that has had bearings pressed in and out a few times may not be perfectly round.

Precision is not critical. The standards are separated by 4mm and 10mm, so any reasonably careful measurement will land you in the right one.
:::

::: faq Q006 | What is the difference between 19mm, 22mm, and 24mm BMX crank spindles?
19mm is the default and what most complete bikes and aftermarket cranks use. 22mm is the common durability upgrade. 24mm is mostly found on race setups with external bearing Euro or BSA bottom brackets.

24mm is not straightforwardly the strongest choice. Because the shell fixes the bearing's outer diameter, a bigger spindle means a bigger bore and less bearing material. You trade spindle strength for bearing strength. Also note 24mm is nominally 15/16 inch, which is 23.81mm, so a caliper reading of 23.8 on a used spindle is correct rather than worn.
:::

::: faq Q004 | Can I run 22mm cranks in a frame that had 19mm cranks?
Yes, in the same frame, by changing the bearings. The frame sets the bearing outer diameter and the cranks set the bore, so you keep the outer diameter your shell requires and buy that bearing with a 22mm bore instead of a 19mm one.

You will also want the spacer set that comes with a bottom bracket kit, since the chainline needs setting up again afterwards.
:::

::: faq Q005 | Can I convert an American bottom bracket to Mid?
Yes. Adapter cups press into the American shell and bring it down to accept the Mid bearing. Since a sealed American bottom bracket already runs that same 41.3mm bearing inside its own cups, you are changing the adapter rather than the bearing.

This is the usual route for putting modern three-piece cranks into an older frame.
:::

::: faq Q007 | Are BMX bottom bracket bearings interchangeable between brands?
Dimensionally, yes. A bottom bracket bearing is defined by three numbers, outer diameter, bore, and width, and any bearing matching those will fit. Profile sells one bearing covering both Mid and American for exactly this reason.

What is not interchangeable is the hardware around the bearing. Spacer kits, cones, and pinch bolt arrangements vary between brands, so keep the parts that came with your bottom bracket.
:::

::: faq Q009 | What size bearings does a Spanish bottom bracket use?
37mm outer diameter and 9mm wide, with the bore matching your spindle, so 19mm or 22mm.

Buy them by those dimensions rather than by a bearing part number. One widely repeated part number for Spanish bearings is a standard 6904, but a real 6904 has a 20mm bore, which does not work for either BMX spindle size. These are a special size and an ordinary bearing supplier will not stock them.
:::

::: faq Q010 | Does bottom bracket shell width matter, 68mm or 73mm?
Less than you would expect. Mid and Spanish bottom bracket kits are sold to cover both widths, with the included spacers making up the difference. Euro is 68mm on BMX frames and 73mm on mountain and dirt jump frames, and there the cups do need to suit the shell.

Measure it while you have the calipers out, but it rarely stops a build.
:::

## Thread pitch {#thread-pitch}

::: quickanswer
**Thread pitch is how far apart the threads are, and two parts with the same diameter and different pitches will destroy each other if forced together.** It is why a bolt that starts to go in can still be the wrong bolt.
:::

Diameter tells you whether a fastener will enter. Pitch tells you whether it belongs there. Imperial threads are counted as threads per inch, so a higher TPI number means finer threads. Metric threads are given as the distance between crests in millimetres, so a smaller number is finer.

The reason this matters on a used BMX is that several of the threads on the bike are close enough in diameter to start, and the damage is done in the first turn or two. That is covered under [cross-threading](/guides/bmx-cranks-and-pedals/#cross-threading), and the rule that follows from it is simple: everything threaded gets started by hand, and nothing gets a wrench until it has turned freely for several turns.
